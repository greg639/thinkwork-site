Challenger's 'Teach, Tailor, Take Control': Most Teams Only Ever Install the First Word

Everyone can recite "Teach, Tailor, Take Control." Almost nobody's team can do the second and third word. Here's why the muscle never gets built.

Every enablement leader I've sat across from can recite it without blinking: Teach, Tailor, Take Control. It's the most successful three-word slogan sales methodology has ever produced, which is exactly the problem. Ask that same leader to pull up a call recording where a rep does the second word, not the first, and watch the confidence drain out of the room in real time.

I've audited enough Challenger rollouts now to know the pattern by heart. Six-figure engagement, a genuinely sharp insight deck, a two-day certification bootcamp, a library of "commercial teaching" one-pagers for every vertical. Reps come out of the room able to deliver the insight pitch cold, word for word, with the cadence the facilitator taught them. Eighteen months later you pull ten calls at random and find the same insight pitch delivered, near-verbatim, to a CFO and a warehouse operations manager on the same afternoon. Nobody tailored anything. Nobody took control of anything. Somebody installed one word out of three and called it a methodology.

Teach is the easy 30%

Teach is a content problem, and content problems are what enablement teams are built to solve. Somebody researched the buyer's industry, found a genuinely surprising point of view, built a deck, trained delivery. That's real work and I'm not knocking it. But it's also the part of Challenger that asks the least of the rep behaviourally. You're asking them to memorise and perform, closer to giving a conference talk than to selling.

Tailor and Take Control are different animals. They require the rep to do something live, under pressure, without a script, using judgement that either exists or doesn't. And here's the part almost nobody says out loud in the retro: most enablement curricula never train the judgement. They assume it turns up once a rep has memorised enough insight decks. It doesn't.

The skill nobody names: reframing someone's own numbers back to them

Tailoring the insight to a specific stakeholder isn't swapping a logo on slide four. It's taking the commercial insight and re-deriving it inside that stakeholder's own economics: their budget line, their KPI, the number their boss will ask about in Monday's leadership meeting. A CFO doesn't want your insight about market disruption. They want to see their own cost-of-goods number moved by it, expressed in their language, built on their assumptions.

That's a specific, learnable, and almost never trained competency: economic reframing. In practice it looks like this. The insight deck says "companies that don't modernise process X lose 12% margin to inefficiency within three years." A rep who can genuinely Tailor takes that stat, asks two or three sharp questions to find this stakeholder's actual margin, actual timeline pressure, actual internal metric, and rebuilds the case live: "on your numbers, that's not 12%, that's closer to £340k a year sitting inside your current process, and you're the one who owns that line in the board deck." That's a different competency to "deliver the insight pitch well." It requires live arithmetic, stakeholder-specific business literacy, and the nerve to say a number back to someone before you're fully sure it will land.

Ask most enablement programmes where this is taught and you get a shrug, or a slide that says "understand your buyer's priorities" with no practice reps, no rubric, and no way to tell a rep who can genuinely do it from one who's guessing well.

Take Control needs proof, not a script

Take Control gets flattened into "be more assertive" in most rollouts, which produces reps who either overcorrect into pushiness or quietly ignore the instruction because it feels risky without backup. The actual mechanism is this: a rep can push back on a stakeholder's assumption and hold a point of tension about price or process comfortably because they've already proven, in the Tailor step, that they understand this person's business better than the stakeholder expected a vendor to. Take Control isn't a personality trait you train with a role-play about "handling objections." It's confidence earned by having just demonstrated real command of someone's numbers. Skip Tailor and Take Control has no foundation to stand on, which is exactly why it's the one that evaporates first in the field.

What gets built vs. what the deal needs

Challenger wordWhat most rollouts actually buildWhat the deal actually needs
TeachAn insight deck plus a delivery scriptFine as built: genuinely the easy third
TailorA slide that says "know your buyer"Live economic reframing: turning a generic stat into this stakeholder's specific number, under time pressure, without a script
Take ControlA role-play on "handling pushback"The confidence to hold tension, earned by having just proven command of the buyer's own numbers

Tailor sits in the middle of that table because it's the hinge. Without it, Teach is theatre and Take Control is bravado.

The fix isn't a longer deck

If your Challenger rollout is stalling at Teach, the answer isn't a refresher on the insight pitch. It's building the reframing skill as its own trained competency, with its own reps, its own feedback, and its own way of grading whether a rep did it well or just talked confidently. That means live drills where reps take a generic insight and re-derive it against a stakeholder profile they've never seen before, graded not on delivery polish but on whether the number they landed on actually maps to that stakeholder's real economics. Use something like the Metrics & Business Case ROI Worksheet as scaffolding for that drill, and the Commercial Insight Reframe Message Map to give reps a repeatable structure for the reframe itself rather than leaving it to improv.

And if you're the one deciding whether reps have actually got there, don't grade on whether they can recite Teach. Grade on whether they can do Tailor cold, in front of a stakeholder profile they've never seen, and know it when you see it, a different measurement problem to "did they pass the certification quiz." A Sales Certification Rubric (Novice-to-Expert Levels) built around observed behaviour, not recall, is the difference between a programme that installs a slogan and one that installs a skill.

Most Challenger rollouts don't fail because the framework is wrong. They fail because everyone quietly agreed the deck was the hard part, and the deck was always the easy 30%.
