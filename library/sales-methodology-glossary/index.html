Sales Methodology Glossary

Plain-English, one-page definitions for every sales methodology and qualification acronym you'll hit researching a framework — MEDDIC to PACT — so you can walk into a vendor call or a leadership debate already speaking the language.

Every acronym and framework name you're likely to hit while researching sales methodology, defined in plain English. Organized roughly from qualification frameworks → sales-process/message frameworks → strategic-account frameworks → quick-hit terms.

Qualification frameworks

MEDDIC — Metrics, Economic Buyer, Decision Criteria, Decision Process, Identify Pain, Champion. The original enterprise-B2B qualification checklist, built at PTC in the 1990s. Forces a rep to quantify pain (Metrics), confirm they're talking to someone who can actually sign (Economic Buyer), understand how the deal will be judged and by whom (Decision Criteria / Decision Process), and secure an internal advocate (Champion). Best for complex, multi-stakeholder enterprise sales.

MEDDPICC — MEDDIC plus Paper Process (legal/procurement/security review steps that can silently stall a "closed-won" deal) and Competition (who else is in the deal, and why you win). The most common variant in modern enterprise SaaS.

MEDDICC — A middle variant: MEDDIC plus Competition, without a separate Paper Process letter. Functionally close to MEDDPICC; teams pick whichever spelling their CRM fields already use.

BANT — Budget, Authority, Need, Timeline. IBM's late-1960s qualification checklist — the oldest framework still in active use. Simple and fast, but leads with budget before pain is established, which can end a conversation before value is proven. Best for transactional or SMB motions with short cycles.

ANUM — Authority, Need, Urgency, Money. A BANT variant that leads with Authority instead of Budget, on the logic that talking to the wrong person makes every other qualifier irrelevant.

SPICED — Situation, Pain, Impact, Critical event, Decision. Developed by Winning by Design for product-led and modern SaaS motions. Leans on "Critical event" (why the buyer must act now) more than a named single decision-maker, which suits flatter, more consensus-driven buying committees.

CHAMP — Challenges, Authority, Money, Prioritization. Reorders BANT to lead with the buyer's challenges instead of budget, and adds "Prioritization" — is this problem even in their top 3 right now?

FAINT — Funds, Authority, Interest, Need, Timing. A top-of-funnel/inbound variant for situations where "Need" isn't obvious yet — "Interest" stands in as an earlier, softer signal than confirmed need.

PACT — Pain, Authority, Consequence, Target profile. A newer framework that emphasizes the consequence of inaction as a qualifier in its own right, not just an implied part of pain.

Sales-process / questioning frameworks

SPIN Selling — Situation, Problem, Implication, Need-payoff questions, in that order. Neil Rackham's 1988 research-backed questioning sequence: establish facts (Situation), surface a problem, make the buyer feel its cost (Implication), then get them to state the value of solving it (Need-payoff) — so the buyer, not the rep, articulates the case for buying.

GAP Selling — Built by Keenan: sells the gap between the buyer's current state and their desired future state. Problem-centric rather than solution-centric — reps are trained to fully diagnose the current-state pain before ever describing the product.

Challenger Sale — Matthew Dixon and Brent Adamson's research (2011) identifying the "Challenger" rep profile as the highest performer: Teach (bring a non-obvious insight), Tailor (to the stakeholder), Take control (of the conversation, including on price). Built around commercial insight rather than relationship-building.

Sandler Selling System — A "no-pressure" philosophy built on the pain funnel (progressively deeper pain questions), up-front contracts (agreeing what happens at the end of every interaction before it starts), and deliberately reversing pressure back onto the buyer ("Why are you telling me this?").

Solution Selling — Michael Bosworth's framework: sell to a diagnosed pain, position the rep as the architect of a "vision" of the solution before ever pitching product specifics.

Value Selling Framework — Quantifies business value/ROI at every stage of the deal, not just at the proposal — the business case is built and refined continuously, not bolted on at the end.

Message and process frameworks (Force Management)

Command of the Message — Builds the narrative: customer problem, differentiated value, and proof — so every rep tells a consistent, compelling story regardless of who's in the room.

Command of the Sale — The process-side counterpart: opportunity management and qualification rigor, commonly paired with MEDDPICC as the qualification engine underneath the message.

Strategic-account frameworks

Target Account Selling (TAS) — A structured approach to mapping and prioritizing accounts and buying influences within a named list of target accounts, historically paired with a formal account plan.

Miller Heiman Strategic Selling — Built around the "Blue Sheet" and four buying influences: Economic (controls the money), User (will use the product), Technical (can say no but not yes — compliance/security/IT), and Coach (internal ally). Useful for mapping who actually needs to say yes on a complex deal.

Conceptual Selling (Miller Heiman) — A companion framework focused on understanding the buyer's own mental "concept" of the solution before presenting anything, so the pitch matches how they've already framed the problem in their own head.

Bonus quick-hit terms

  • Economic Buyer — the person with the authority and budget to say yes and make it happen; not necessarily the most senior person in the room.
  • Champion — an internal advocate with influence who wants you to win and will sell on your behalf when you're not in the room.
  • Power Sponsor — a champion one level up: someone senior enough to unblock the deal, not just support it.
  • Compelling Event — a specific, dated reason the buyer must act now (contract renewal, regulatory deadline, budget cycle) rather than "sometime."
  • Mutual Action Plan (MAP) — a shared, dated document co-owned by buyer and seller listing every step needed to close, used to keep both sides honest about timeline.
  • Multithreading — building relationships with multiple stakeholders in an account, not just one contact — so the deal survives a single champion leaving or going quiet.
