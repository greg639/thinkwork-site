Culture Isn't a Deck. It's Whoever You Promoted Last.

Values slides don't set culture. The last three promotion and termination decisions your company made did, and they were probably sending a different message than the deck.

Every sales org I've walked into has a culture deck. Values on a slide, usually four or five words apiece, usually printed large near reception so visitors can admire them on the way to the loo. Integrity. Customer obsession. Winning as one team. Nobody inside the building can recite these off the top of their head, including the person who wrote them. And none of it matters, because the deck isn't your culture. Your culture is whoever got promoted last quarter, whoever got shown the door last quarter, and whether either decision actually matched what the deck says.

I've sat on the other side of enough promotion committees to tell you this isn't cynicism, it's mechanics. Employees are excellent pattern-matchers on incentive, and terrible readers of stated intent. They watch what gets rewarded and they copy it, in about one review cycle. A poster on the wall carries zero enforcement weight. A promotion carries all of it: it says, in public, in front of the whole floor, "this is the behaviour we want more of, and here's the pay rise and title to prove it." Nobody has ever quietly ignored a promotion the way they quietly ignore a Friday all-hands values reminder.

The deck is aspiration. The promotion is policy.

Values statements are usually written by someone in marketing, HR, or the CEO on an offsite, with the best of intentions and none of the use over pay or title. Promotion and termination decisions are made by frontline sales leaders under quota pressure, usually without anyone in the room asking whether this matches what we say we stand for. Those are two entirely different governance processes producing two entirely different signals, and the second one wins every time because it comes with money attached.

This is why you'll find sales orgs with "customer obsession" on the wall that just promoted the rep who oversold scope to hit a number, dumping an angry, badly-fit customer on the CS team six weeks later. It's why you'll find "one team" on the wall next to a newly minted manager who got there by hoarding the best leads and never once helping onboard the SDRs underneath him. Reps don't need to read the fine print to know which of these is actually rewarded. They were in the room. They saw the applause.

Run the audit before you touch the deck

Skip the workshop where you wordsmith the values again. Do this instead, it takes twenty minutes and it's more honest than anything a facilitator will get out of your leadership team in a full day offsite.

Write down your last three promotions into management or a senior IC role. For each one, in one plain sentence, describe in behavioural terms what actually got them promoted: not "hit number," but the specific thing they did. Then do the same for your last three exits or PIPs. Then hold each sentence up against your stated values and ask, honestly, does this match?

What the deck saysWhat the promotion actually rewardedThe real message the floor received
"Customer obsession"Closed faster by overselling scope; CS now firefighting a bad-fit accountSpeed to close beats fit to need
"One team"Solo top performer who hoarded leads and never mentored SDRs made managerHoarding wins, sharing is for people who miss quota
"Integrity"Rep who flagged a slipping deal early and missed forecast got PIP'd; rep who sandbagged and surfaced bad news at the eleventh hour kept their job and their bonusDon't tell me bad news early: hide it better
"Winning together"Manager promoted to regional lead despite two direct reports quietly transferring out to escape himNumbers cover for how you treat people

If your table looks anything like this, you don't have a culture problem you can fix with better slide design. You have a promotion-criteria problem, and it's been running the org quietly for longer than the deck has existed.

Fix the mechanism, not the poster

The honest fix isn't a rebrand of the values. It's making the actual promotion and termination criteria explicit, behavioural, and checked against the values before the decision goes public, not after someone asks why the new VP is universally disliked by his old team.

That means promotion criteria that name specific competencies rather than "exceeds quota, eighteen months' tenure." If you don't have that written down anywhere your leadership team can actually apply consistently, the Sales Leadership Competency Scorecard is a reasonable place to start: it forces you to define what "ready for management" looks like in behaviours, not just in bookings.

It also means checking your comp plan, because comp is a far more honest document than your values deck: it's the one text every rep reads carefully, every quarter, without being asked to. If your comp plan pays out identically whether a rep gets there by coaching juniors or by cannibalising their own pipeline, don't be surprised when the culture looks like the second thing. The Sales Compensation Plan Design Toolkit is worth a look before your next comp refresh, specifically to check whether the behaviours you say you want are the ones the plan actually pays for.

None of this is about running a kinder promotion process for its own sake. It's about closing the gap between what you say the culture is and what you've actually been paying for, because your reps have already done that maths. They did it the week the last promotion went out.

If you want to know what your culture actually is, don't read the deck. Read your next promotion memo before you sign off on it. That's about to become the real one, whether you meant it to or not.
