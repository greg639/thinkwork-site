Stop Building Onboarding by Reverse-Engineering Your Best Rep

Shadowing your top performer feels like the obvious way to build a curriculum. It usually teaches new hires someone else's compensations, not the skill underneath them.

Every enablement team has done this at least once: pull the top rep, record twenty of their calls, extract the patterns, and turn those patterns into the new-hire curriculum. It feels rigorous. You're not guessing at what good looks like, you're copying it directly from someone who's provably good at the job. It's also, most of the time, teaching new hires the wrong thing entirely.

The habit you're copying isn't the skill that's working

Here's the part that gets missed. A star performer's visible habits are frequently a workaround for a weakness somewhere else, not a transferable technique. I've sat with reps who talk fast and interrupt prospects constantly: a habit that looks like poor listening on tape, and would get flagged as a gap in anyone else's review. But this particular rep is exceptional at reading urgency and creating it in real time; the interrupting is how they keep momentum on a call where hesitation would let the prospect talk themselves out of the deal. Extract "interrupts frequently" as a best practice from that call library, teach it to twelve new hires who don't have that rep's urgency instinct, and you've just trained a cohort to talk over prospects for no reason at all. The habit was never the skill. It was a patch over a gap the rep had already solved a different way, and the patch only works in the presence of the thing it's patching.

This happens constantly and quietly, because star reps are rarely aware of their own compensations. Ask a top performer why they do something and you'll get a confident, plausible-sounding answer that is frequently wrong: not because they're lying, but because most expert behaviour runs below conscious awareness. The rep who's brilliant at negotiation might tell you it's because they "build rapport first," when the tape shows the actual lever is that they anchor pricing conversations with a specific, named trade-off structure the rapport-building has nothing to do with. Curriculum built from what the rep says they do, or even from what a naive read of the tape shows they do, inherits the confusion along with the genuine skill.

What a competency-standard approach does differently

The alternative isn't more careful shadowing. It's decoupling curriculum design from any single person's call history entirely, and building from a named competency standard instead: a defined list of the skills the role requires, each with its own description of what proficient execution actually looks like, independent of any one rep's idiosyncratic route to it.

ApproachWhere the standard comes fromMain failure mode
Reverse-engineer the top repOne person's call recordingsTeaches compensations alongside genuine skill, with no way to tell them apart
Reverse-engineer the whole top-quartileMultiple people's call recordingsSlightly more robust, but still teaches whatever's common across compensating styles rather than the underlying competency
Build from a named competency standardA defined skill taxonomy, independently authoredRequires more upfront work; nothing to reverse-engineer, so it demands actual definition

A competency standard names the skill first: objection reframing, discovery-questioning depth, multi-threading, negotiation anchoring: and defines what proficient, developing, and advanced execution look like for each, in behavioural terms that don't depend on any one person's delivery style. Then you go looking for examples of that named competency being executed well, from wherever it shows up, including your top performer's calls when they demonstrate it cleanly and excluding the moments where they're compensating for something else. The New Rep Certification Scorecard you build off the back of that standard is testing whether a new hire can execute the named skill, not whether they've absorbed one person's tics.

This also fixes a second, quieter problem with the top-rep-shadowing model: it only ever produces one archetype. If your top performer is a fast-talking urgency-creator, your curriculum trains everyone toward fast-talking urgency-creation, and you've built an onboarding program that structurally fails anyone whose natural style doesn't fit that mould: even though a quieter, more methodical rep could hit the same proficiency on the same named competencies through a completely different route. A standard built around named skills has room for more than one way to demonstrate mastery. A standard built around one person's tape has room for exactly one.

What to actually do with your top rep's call library

Don't throw the recordings out: they're still useful, just not as the source of the curriculum. Use them as evidence against a standard you've already defined elsewhere: does this call demonstrate proficient objection reframing, yes or no, independent of the surrounding style? Build your Call Shadowing Checklist for New Reps around specific, named moments that demonstrate a competency cleanly, flagged as "this segment shows proficient negotiation anchoring" rather than "watch this whole call and absorb it." The tape becomes an illustration of a standard that exists independently of it, instead of the standard itself.

Your best rep got good despite some of their own habits, not entirely because of them. A curriculum that can't tell the difference is going to hand your new hires the compensations for free and make them find the actual skill the hard way, same as your star rep did: except your star rep had years to work it out by accident, and your new hires have ninety days and a quota.
