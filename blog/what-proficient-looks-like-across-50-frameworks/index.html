What 'Proficient' Actually Looks Like Across 50 Sales Frameworks

Tenure predicts proficiency far less than everyone assumes, the data across a fifty-framework observed corpus says something less comfortable about who's actually good.

Ask a VP of Sales how they know a rep is ready for the next rung, and most will point at a number: four years closing enterprise deals, so they're enterprise-ready. Tenure is the laziest proxy on the org chart, and it survives because nobody's had the data to embarrass it. We have the data now, and it isn't kind.

The Mastery Standard grades calls, not CVs. Across the corpus behind it: 50 sales frameworks, 54 competencies, scored from transcribed calls at 100-plus companies, blind to who's being graded, tenure explains almost none of the spread in proficiency. The correlation between years in a quota-carrying seat and graded proficiency on core competencies sits around 0.2. That's not "weak but directionally useful." That's close to noise.

The number that should worry your promotion committee

Pull every rep in the corpus with four or more years of continuous quota-carrying experience and look at what they actually graded on the competencies their title assumes they've mastered. Just over a third of them graded Novice or Developing, the two lowest of five bands, on at least one competency core to their role.

That's not a rep who's rusty on one niche skill. That's a senior AE who, on tape, still can't get past a champion to an economic buyer, or a "closer" whose negotiation behaviour on the call is concession-then-hope rather than anything structured.

Where the gap concentrates

Not every competency shows the same tenure blindness. Some genuinely do improve with reps: pipeline hygiene, CRM discipline, basic rapport. Others don't move at all, because nobody ever corrected the habit and the job never forced it. Ranked by size of the tenure/proficiency gap, worst first:

RankCompetency% of 4+yr reps grading Novice/DevelopingWhy tenure doesn't fix it
1Economic buyer access41%Champion-only conversations still close often enough on tape to never get corrected: the rep never has to feel the miss
2Negotiation under margin pressure38%Most "negotiation" a rep does is concede in a nicer order; nobody scores the concession pattern, only the discount and the signature
3Discovery depth (beyond the first layer)33%First-layer questions get first-layer answers that still produce a deal often enough to feel like a working method
4Objection handling under live pressure29%Scripted rebuttals cover the common objections; the skill gap only shows on the objections the script doesn't anticipate

Economic buyer access tops the list for a specific reason: it's the competency most easily hidden by a good champion. A rep can run four years of deals through a strong internal advocate, close a reasonable share of them, and never once be forced to sit across from the person who signs the cheque. The habit isn't corrected because it isn't visibly broken, it's just capped. Deals that would have expanded, or closed faster, or survived a champion leaving mid-cycle, quietly don't. Nobody puts that in a QBR as a skills problem. It gets filed as "market conditions" or "champion went dark."

Negotiation ranks second for a similar reason, with a nastier detail attached. Most manager review of negotiation looks at outcome: final price, final terms, not behaviour. A rep who concedes 15% in the first exchange and calls it "flexibility" looks identical, on a CRM record, to a rep who held the line and traded concessions for commitments. The tape tells them apart in the first ninety seconds. The pipeline report never does.

Why "one year of experience, four times" is a real phenomenon

The reps who close the gap fastest aren't the ones with the most calls behind them: they're the ones who got specific, graded feedback on a competency early and adjusted. The reps who plateau are usually running the same behavioural loop on repeat: same discovery questions, same objection responses, same negotiation posture, year after year, with volume standing in for growth. Four years of that isn't four years of development. It's one year, repeated four times, with the serial numbers filed off.

This is the part that should change how a promotion committee reads a CV. "Four years closing mid-market" is a tenure claim. It tells you nothing about whether the underlying competency: discovery, qualification, negotiation, multithreading: actually progressed across that time, or calcified in month six and coasted from there.

What to check before tenure gets you the promotion

If you're deciding who moves up, the fix isn't complicated, it's just a different question. Instead of "how long have they done this," ask "what does the tape say about the specific competencies this next role demands." A rep moving into a strategic-account patch needs graded proficiency in economic buyer access and multithreading, not four more years of the champion-only motion that got them here. A rep moving into a coaching role needs to demonstrate the skill on tape before you hand them the responsibility of teaching it to six other people.

The Sales Career Level Matrix (IC1 → Principal/Enterprise) is useful here precisely because it separates level from tenure, it names the competency bar for each rung rather than the years typically associated with it. Pair it with something that tracks progress on the specific gap, like an Individual Development Plan (IDP) Template for Sales Reps, and you've replaced a guess with a measurement.

And if economic buyer access or objection handling under real pressure is the specific hole, don't wait for the next live deal to find out if the fix worked. Drill it first, the Objection-Handling Skill Practice Swipe File exists so the first rep to test a new response isn't a six-figure account.

Tenure isn't worthless. It's just not a proficiency score, and every organisation that treats it as one is promoting on the wrong axis. The corpus doesn't argue that experience never helps, it argues that experience without correction just gets more confident at the same ceiling.
