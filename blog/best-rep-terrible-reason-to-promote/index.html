Your Best Rep Is a Terrible Reason to Promote Someone

The instincts that make someone a great individual seller are frequently the opposite of what makes someone a great developer of other sellers, and most promotion committees never test for the difference.

Every sales floor has one. Forty percent over quota for three straight quarters, unbothered by a hostile prospect, closes on a Friday what everyone else is still qualifying on the following Tuesday. When the manager seat opens up, there's rarely a debate. There's a coronation. I've sat in on this decision more times than I can count across three CCO roles, and by my own rough count, something like six in ten of those promotions failed within eighteen months. Not spectacularly, quietly. The new manager got moved back onto the floor, or performance-managed out within a year, or hung on while the team's numbers slid and everyone agreed not to say so out loud. The talent that earned the promotion was almost never the talent the job actually needed. Nobody had checked whether it was.

The instinct that wins deals is the instinct that sinks teams

Here is the uncomfortable part: it isn't that your best rep lacks talent for management. It's that the specific talent making them a brilliant closer is frequently the inverse of what a manager needs, and promotion committees treat quota attainment as a proxy for a completely different skill set without ever testing whether the correlation exists.

Take a rep who's spent four years learning to trust her own read of a room: she hears an objection, she knows in half a second whether it's real or a stall, and she moves. That instinct is why she's brilliant on a call. Put her in a 1:1 with a rep who's making the same mistake for the third straight week, and that instinct becomes a liability. She doesn't diagnose the other person's specific, differently-wired instinct. She reaches for her own playbook and hands it over: "Just say it like this." The rep parrots the line once, badly, on a call that doesn't match the template, and nothing has actually been taught. Ventriloquism isn't coaching. It produces people who can recite one sentence and fall apart the moment the prospect deviates from the script.

Or take the instinct to own the outcome personally, the thing that makes a great closer refuse to let a deal slip on someone else's failure. The manager version of that same instinct is sitting in a 1:1 watching a rep botch a close you know, with total certainty, you would have saved: and doing nothing, because the rep needs to make that mistake and recover from it themselves, twice more, before the lesson lands. Most brilliant individual closers have never been asked to sit with that kind of controlled failure. Their entire career has trained them to intervene the second something looks wrong. Management asks them to do the opposite, repeatedly, for months, with someone else's number on the line.

None of this means your best rep can't manage. Some of the best sales leaders I've worked with were also excellent individual sellers. But that overlap is a coincidence you have to test for, not a default you get to assume.

The test nobody runs

Most promotion committees run exactly one test: attainment against quota, looked at over enough quarters to feel rigorous. That's a test of a completely different skill. If you want to know whether someone can coach, test coaching, directly, before the offer is final and irreversible.

Three things I'd actually run, none of which take more than a couple of weeks:

  1. Hand them a real call recording, not their own, and ask them to name the one skill gap. Not "how would you have closed it," which just re-tests their own selling instinct. Ask them to identify a specific, nameable competency the rep is missing, and sketch how they'd close that gap over two weeks. If the answer is a tactic ("tell them to mention the case study earlier"), they're still thinking like a closer. If the answer names a skill and a repeatable way to build it, that's the signal you're looking for.
  2. Put them in a trial coaching seat before you make it permanent. Sixty to ninety days shadowing a manager's 1:1s and calls, giving live feedback under supervision, before the title change and the comp change happen. This costs you almost nothing and tells you more than a year of watching their quota attainment ever will.
  3. Ask them about a rep they didn't respect. Listen for contempt versus diagnosis. Coaches who talk about a struggling rep with genuine curiosity about what's actually wrong tend to develop people. Coaches who talk about them like dead weight tend to become Enforcers, fast.

If you want this formalised rather than improvised in the room, build a version of your hiring rubric for it: the same discipline behind a Sales Candidate Scorecard: The 7-Competency Hiring Rubric applies just as well to internal promotion, and most committees never bother writing one down for a decision that costs far more when it goes wrong.

Give your best rep somewhere else to go

Part of why this promotion happens so automatically is structural: for a long time, "manager" was the only growth path a great rep could see. Fix that and you remove most of the pressure to promote someone who isn't ready.

Build an individual-contributor ladder that goes as high as your management ladder: title, comp, and status attached to selling mastery itself, not to headcount managed. A Sales Career Level Matrix (IC1 → Principal/Enterprise) gives you the scaffolding to make that real rather than cosmetic, and it gives your best rep a legitimate answer to "what's next" that isn't "manage people you've never been tested on managing." Pair it with an Individual Development Plan (IDP) Template for Sales Reps so the growth path has actual content in it, not just a bigger number on a business card.

Your best rep already has a job they're excellent at. Promoting them into one you haven't tested them for isn't a reward. It's a bet you're making with someone else's career, dressed up as recognition.
