What Slow Rampers Actually Have in Common (It Isn't Aptitude)

Two reps hired the same week, same background, same territory. One ramped in six weeks. One took five months. The gap wasn't talent.

Two reps started on the same Monday last spring: same SDR-to-AE track record, same territory split down the postcode, same onboarding cohort, same manager. Six weeks later, one had a signed deal and a forecast that looked like the top of the leaderboard. Five months later, the other still hadn't hit "ramped" by any definition the org used, generous or strict. The manager, explaining the gap in a QBR review, used the word "aptitude" twice in one sentence about two people who'd scored within two points of each other on the same hiring assessment. Aptitude wasn't the story. It almost never is.

The pattern, repeated enough times to stop calling it coincidence

Across cohorts we've assessed, the slow ramper is rarely someone who's generally behind. They're someone who's specifically behind on one thing, or occasionally two, and everything downstream compounds off that single gap until it looks like a broad deficit from the outside.

Rep A in that pair had one hole: no ability to multi-thread past a single champion. Every deal ran through one contact. When that contact went quiet for three weeks: new priorities, a reorg, nothing sinister: the deal died with them, and Rep A had no fallback because no one had ever taught them to build one. Rep B, on a different desk we assessed the same quarter, had a different single hole: jumped straight into a scripted pitch before establishing what the buyer actually cared about, so every subsequent objection was a surprise instead of something discovery should have surfaced. Neither of these is a general selling deficiency. Both are named, specific, trainable gaps that happened to be invisible to a manager watching for "does this person seem sharp on calls."

Where the gaps actually cluster

In the observation data across the cohorts we've worked with, slow-ramp cases trace back to a small number of repeat offenders far more often than to a broad skill deficit:

  1. Qualification discipline. Chasing every inbound lead as if it's equally likely to close, with no framework for disqualifying early. This is the single biggest time-sink we see, because it doesn't just slow one deal, it eats the hours that should have gone into the 20% of pipeline actually worth the effort.
  2. Multi-threading and stakeholder mapping. One champion, no plan B. The deal dies the moment that person changes role, goes quiet, or gets overruled by someone the rep never met.
  3. Objection handling under real pressure. Fine on the easy, expected objections. Freezes or over-concedes the moment a senior buyer pushes on price or timing with language the rep hasn't drilled.
  4. Discovery depth. Collects surface answers and treats discovery as a checklist to clear rather than an investigation to run, so the pitch that follows misses the actual point of pain: and the rep can't tell you why the deal stalled, because they never found out what mattered in the first place.

They're ranked in that order because of where they sit in the funnel: a qualification gap wastes the most hours before anything else even has a chance to go wrong, which makes it the costliest to leave unnamed. Discovery depth sits last because its damage is real but slower to show up: it takes a full cycle before the missed pain point surfaces as a lost deal, which makes it the easiest of the four to misdiagnose as something else entirely.

Why "more reps" doesn't fix it

The standard response to a slow ramper is more of the same medicine: another shadowing block, another module bolted onto week nine, a generic "let's get you more reps in." That instinct treats the rep as globally behind, when they're precisely behind on one thing. More repetitions of a pattern that has a specific hole in it just repeat the hole with more confidence behind it. A rep who doesn't multi-thread doesn't fix that by running twenty more single-threaded deals, they get faster at a habit you don't want them to keep.

What actually cuts ramp time: naming it in week two

The single highest-impact move available to a sales leader isn't a better onboarding curriculum. It's a structured assessment in week two: two or three real or role-played calls, scored against named competencies rather than a manager's overall impression, that names the exact gap before it's had time to compound. Catch "no qualification framework" in week two instead of discovering it via a blown deal in month three, and you can drill that one skill for two weeks with a mentor rather than losing an entire quarter to a pattern nobody named. The Core 12 Sales Competency Framework is a reasonable starting point if you don't already have named competencies to assess against: the point isn't the specific list, it's having one at all, so week-two feedback can point at something concrete instead of "keep at it."

Once the gap is named, the fix is deliberately narrow. Build a two-week plan targeting the one skill, not a refreshed version of the general curriculum everyone else is getting: an Individual Development Plan (IDP) Template for Sales Reps works better here than a training calendar, because it's built to track one named target rather than a syllabus. Re-assess the same competency at week four. If it's closed, the rep continues on the normal track. If it isn't, escalate to focused 1:1 coaching on that specific skill: not more onboarding content, which by week four is solving a problem this rep no longer has.

It's worth watching for the gap earlier than week two if you can. A rep with close to zero activity-to-outcome conversion by week three is showing you the pattern already; a Time-to-First-Deal Tracker Template catches that signal before a manager's gut does, which is exactly the point.

The org that figured this out stopped asking "why is this rep slow" and started asking "which one thing is this rep missing." The answer, uncomfortably often, was something a two-call review would have caught in week two: sitting in plain sight the whole time, waiting for someone to ask a narrower question.
