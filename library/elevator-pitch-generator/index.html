Elevator Pitch Generator

Answer four prompts about your offer and your buyer, run them through the assembly formula, and get a tight, tailored 30-second pitch you can say out loud immediately.

What's inside

  • The 4 input prompts (buyer, problem, mechanism, proof)
  • The assembly formula that turns 4 answers into one pitch
  • 3 situational variants: networking pitch, cold-call opener, elevator/random-encounter version
  • Word-count and pacing targets for a true 30-second delivery
  • Jargon filter — banned words list
  • 3 fully worked examples across different personas/industries
  • Self-scoring rubric (6 checks) to grade your own draft
  • Delivery notes: pause points, and the one follow-up question every version must end on

Four questions in, one 30-second pitch out.

Step 1 — Answer these four prompts

Write one sentence for each. Don't polish yet — just get the raw material down.

  1. Who is your buyer, specifically? (role + the trigger that makes them care right now — not “businesses,” a real title and situation)
  2. What problem do they have today that's costing them something measurable? (time, money, risk, reputation)
  3. What do you actually do about it — the mechanism, not the feature list? (the “how,” in one clause)
  4. What's the proof it works? (a number, a name, a result — not an adjective)

Step 2 — The Assembly Formula

“I help [Prompt 1 — buyer] who are dealing with [Prompt 2 — problem] to [Prompt 3 — mechanism], so that [outcome derived from Prompt 4 — proof]. Most recently that looked like [one concrete proof point].”

That's it — four prompts, one formula, roughly 45–60 words when spoken at a natural pace (that's your 30 seconds).

Step 3 — Jargon Filter

Strike any of these from your draft — they signal generic sales-speak and buyers tune out the moment they hear them:

  • “Synergy,” “leverage,” “solutions,” “best-in-class,” “end-to-end,” “robust,” “seamless,” “cutting-edge,” “game-changing,” “disrupt”
  • Any sentence that would be equally true if you swapped in a competitor's name
  • Any acronym your buyer wouldn't use themselves in the first meeting

Step 4 — Three Situational Variants

A. Networking version (they asked “what do you do?”)

Use the full formula, all four sentences, relaxed pace. End with a question, not a pitch close: “Does that sound like something [their company/role] deals with, or is that not really on your radar?”

B. Cold-call opener (first 15 seconds of a call)

Compress to Prompts 1+2+3 only, drop the proof line, and go straight into a permission question: “The reason I'm calling — [1-sentence version of buyer + problem + mechanism]. Did I catch you at a slightly OK time, or is this a bad moment?”

C. Elevator/random-encounter version (10 seconds, no ask)

One sentence only — Prompt 1 + Prompt 2 collapsed into a single clause, then stop talking: “I work with [buyer] on [problem], mostly around [mechanism in 3–4 words].” Let them ask the follow-up. Do not volunteer more.

Word Count & Pacing Targets

  • Full version (A): 45–65 words = ~28–32 seconds spoken at conversational pace (~130 wpm)
  • Compressed (B): 20–30 words = ~10–14 seconds
  • One-liner (C): 12–18 words = ~6–8 seconds

If your draft is longer, cut adjectives before you cut facts — proof points survive, describing words don't.

Worked Examples

Example 1 — B2B SaaS, sold to RevOps leaders

“I help RevOps leaders at 200–1,000 person B2B companies who are losing forecast accuracy to messy CRM data clean and standardize their pipeline without a re-implementation project. Most recently that looked like a client going from 61% to 89% forecast accuracy in one quarter.”

Example 2 — Recruitment agency, sold to hiring managers

“I help engineering hiring managers who are burning six-plus weeks per senior hire get a shortlist of pre-vetted, interview-ready candidates in under 10 days. Most recently that looked like filling a Staff Engineer role in 8 days for a client who'd had the req open for two months.”

Example 3 — Boutique consultancy, sold to founders

“I help founder-led B2B companies who are trying to professionalize their sales motion for the first time put in a repeatable process without hiring a VP of Sales they can't afford yet. Most recently that looked like a 12-person company doubling qualified pipeline in a quarter using their existing team.”

Self-Scoring Rubric — grade your draft out of 6

  • Names a specific buyer role, not a vague market
  • States a problem with a measurable consequence (time/money/risk), not a vague pain
  • Describes the mechanism in a clause, not a feature list
  • Includes one real proof point (number, name, or result)
  • Contains zero words from the jargon filter
  • Ends on a question or natural pause, never a hard close

Score 6/6 before you use it live. Anything under 4/6, go back to Step 1.

Delivery Notes

  • Pause after Prompt 2 (the problem) — let it land before you move to the mechanism.
  • Slow down on the proof point; numbers said fast sound made up.
  • Every version ends on a question that invites the other person to talk — the pitch is a door, not a monologue.

How to use it

Fill in the four prompts in one sitting, run them through the assembly formula, cut anything from the jargon filter, then say it out loud and time it — adjust for the situational variant (networking, cold call, or elevator) you'll actually use it in.
