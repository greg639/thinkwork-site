Which Sales Qualification Framework Fits You? Quiz

A 12-question, self-scoring quiz that matches your actual deal cycle and buying committee shape to the qualification framework that fits it — MEDDIC, SPIN, BANT, or GAP Selling — instead of whichever one your last manager happened to like.

What's inside

  • 12 diagnostic questions covering deal cycle length, buying committee shape, budget origin, and buyer problem-awareness
  • A consistent A/B/C/D scoring column mapped to BANT, SPIN, MEDDIC, and GAP Selling
  • A tally table to count your answers
  • A tie-breaker rule for when two frameworks score within one point of each other
  • A one-paragraph 'best fit for' profile for each of the four frameworks
  • A 'when to blend two frameworks' note for reps who run more than one motion
  • Guidance on how often to re-take it as your deal mix changes

Most reps run whatever framework their first sales manager taught them, forever, regardless of whether it fits the deals they're actually working. Answer these 12 questions honestly — based on your actual pipeline, not your best deal or your worst one — then tally your letters.

Each question has four options. Pick the one that's most true most of the time.

Q1. How long is your average sales cycle, first call to signed contract? A) Under 30 days — deals move fast once budget and authority are confirmed B) 1–3 months — enough time to walk the buyer through their own problem C) 3–9 months — enterprise cycle with multiple approval stages D) It genuinely varies — the real driver is the size of the gap between where they are and where they need to be, not the calendar

Q2. What does your buying committee usually look like? A) 1–2 people, usually with signing authority B) 3–4 people, but one clear champion drives the conversation C) 5–8+ people across departments, including a named economic buyer, legal, and security D) The "committee" is loose — the real work is figuring out who owns the problem and who owns the budget for closing it

Q3. How aware is the buyer of their problem on the first call? A) They already know exactly what they want — this is a replacement or renewal B) They sense something's wrong but haven't connected it to a root cause yet C) They've already quantified the problem and have a business case underway D) They think they're fine — your job is to show them the gap between assumed state and actual state

Q4. Where does budget for this purchase come from? A) It's already allocated; the purchase was planned before you showed up B) It gets created in the conversation once they feel the cost of doing nothing C) It exists in a formal budget cycle, and you need the economic buyer to release it D) It's contested — your champion has to build an internal case to move money toward closing the gap you've surfaced

Q5. What actually kills your deals most often? A) Failure to confirm timeline or authority up front — deals just go quiet B) The prospect never fully "feels" the implication of the problem, so urgency fades C) Legal, security, or procurement review derails a deal that was verbally won D) The champion can't articulate the gap (or its cost) to the rest of the buying committee, so it stalls internally

Q6. How does your business case need to land to get a yes? A) Price and fit are enough — there's a felt need already B) The buyer needs to say the pain out loud, in their own words, before they'll move C) A CFO or VP needs a defensible, quantified number tied to specific metrics D) You need a clear before/after: current state, future state, and the dollar size of the gap between them

Q7. What's your typical deal size (ACV)? A) Under $10K B) $10K–$50K C) $50K+, enterprise, often multi-year D) A wide range — you sell the same motion at very different altitudes

Q8. How do you get to the economic buyer? A) You sell directly to the person who signs B) You start with an end user and earn your way up C) You have to independently identify and win over an economic buyer you don't yet know D) Your champion has to bring the economic buyer to the table once the gap is proven

Q9. How much diagnostic questioning does the sale actually require? A) Almost none — the buyer states the requirement and you respond to it B) A structured line of Situation → Problem → Implication → Need-payoff questions C) Some — mostly to validate metrics and criteria the buyer already suspects D) Heavy — the whole first call is mapping current state vs. desired future state and quantifying what sits between them

Q10. How complex is procurement, legal, and security review? A) Simple — a card or a PO closes it B) Light — one approver, fast turnaround C) Heavy — redlines, security review, multi-signature sign-off (a real Paper Process) D) Variable — sometimes trivial, sometimes heavy, and it's rarely mapped until it's already a problem

Q11. What's your instinctive first qualifying question on a new call? A) "Are you the person who'd actually make this decision?" B) "What happens if this doesn't get solved?" C) "What's this costing you today, in numbers you'd defend to your own CFO?" D) "Where are you today, and where do you actually need to be?"

Q12. What's the shape of your stalled pipeline? A) Deals move fast or die fast; there's little "slow maybe" B) Deals stall because the prospect never fully owns the problem as urgent C) Deals stall in legal/procurement after the buyer has already said yes D) Deals stall because no one — including you — ever nailed down the size of the gap or who's accountable for closing it

Scoring

Count how many times you picked each letter:

LetterCountFramework
A___ / 12BANT
B___ / 12SPIN Selling
C___ / 12MEDDIC
D___ / 12GAP Selling

Your highest count is your primary-fit framework.

Tie-breaker: If two letters are within 1 point of each other, use your Q3 answer (problem awareness) as the decider — it's the single highest-leverage differentiator between these four frameworks. A/B ties lean BANT if the cycle is genuinely short (Q1=A); B/D ties lean GAP Selling if your deal size varies (Q7=D); C/D ties lean MEDDIC if your committee has a formal Paper Process (Q10=C).

What each framework is actually for

BANT (Budget, Authority, Need, Timeline) — built for speed. Best when the buyer already knows what they want, the committee is small, and your job is fast disqualification, not diagnosis. Weakest in anything with a real buying committee or a latent problem.

SPIN Selling (Situation, Problem, Implication, Need-payoff) — a questioning discipline, not a full deal framework. Best when the buyer senses a problem but hasn't connected it to consequences yet; your job is to get them to say the implication out loud before you pitch anything.

MEDDIC (Metrics, Economic Buyer, Decision Criteria, Decision Process, Identify Pain, Champion) — built for complex, multi-stakeholder, enterprise sales with a real Paper Process. Overkill for a two-person, 30-day deal; essential once legal, security, and procurement are real gates.

GAP Selling (current state → future state → the gap → the cost of the gap) — best when the buyer underestimates their own problem and the sale hinges on making the size of the gap undeniable, across a wide range of deal sizes and stakeholder counts.

When to blend

Most experienced reps end up running two frameworks stacked, not one in isolation. The most common combination: SPIN's questioning technique to surface the problem, MEDDIC's structure to qualify and forecast what you heard. If your scores show a close B/C split, that's not a failed quiz — that's your actual motion, and you should build call plans that ask SPIN-style questions but log MEDDIC-style fields.

Re-take this quiz whenever your deal mix shifts materially — a move upmarket, a new segment, or a new product line with a different buying process will change your answers.

How to use it

Answer all 12 questions based on your real, current pipeline (not your best or worst deal), tally the letters, and use the framework with the highest count as your primary qualification structure for the next quarter — re-take it if your deal mix shifts.
