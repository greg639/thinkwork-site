What to Actually Ask For in Your First 30 Days as a New Rep

Most new reps wait to be told what to do. The ones who ramp fastest ask for three specific things their manager won't offer by default.

Nobody hands a new rep a map. They hand you a laptop, a login to five systems nobody explained properly, a quota, and a Slack channel called #wins where the senior reps post GIFs every time they close something six figures larger than anything you can currently picture landing. That's onboarding at most companies: absorb the vibe, shadow some calls, ask questions when you're stuck, and hope a curriculum turns up before your manager starts wondering why your numbers still look like everyone's do in month one.

Some reps ramp in half the time. Not because they're more talented. Because in their first week they ask for three specific things almost no manager volunteers unprompted — volunteering them is real work, and most managers are heads-down on their own number, not yours.

The default ramp is a spectator sport

The standard new-rep experience is passive by design, not malice. You get dropped into a rotation of call shadows, a product deck, a CRM login, and a manager who says "just get comfortable" for the first month. Comfort isn't a skill. It's the absence of pressure, and pressure is the only thing that reveals whether you can actually run a discovery call or you've just memorised the questions on a cheat sheet.

The reps who ramp fast treat their manager as a resource to extract structure from, not a source of vibes to absorb. That means asking for things a busy manager will happily give you if you ask directly, but will never build for you unprompted, because building it takes an afternoon they don't have unless someone forces the issue.

The three asks, in the order to make them

AskWhat it replacesWhy it goes in this order
1. A written map of what "good" looks like at each skill tierVague adjectives — "strong," "needs work," "getting there"You can't use feedback you get in week 3 if you don't have a scale to place it on in week 1
2. Graded feedback on real calls, not vibes"That call sounded good!" after your manager half-listened between meetingsOnce you have the map, you need actual coordinates — where you sit on it today
3. A say in which competency you work on nextWhatever the generic 30-60-90 template says everyone does in week 6Once you know where you're weak, you can argue for sequencing that fixes your specific gap instead of the average new hire's gap

Ask one: get the tiers in writing. Most sales competencies — discovery questioning, objection reframing, negotiation, multi-threading — exist on a real progression from novice to proficient to advanced. Almost no manager writes this down for a new hire; they carry it in their head from years of watching reps develop, and they assume you'll absorb it by osmosis. Ask directly: "Can you write down what 'proficient' at discovery actually sounds like, versus what I'm doing now?" A five-tier ladder — novice, developing, proficient, advanced, mastery — for each of the four or five skills your role actually leans on turns "you're doing fine" into something you can act on. If your manager can't produce this off the top of their head, that's useful information too: it means the org hasn't defined its own standard, and you should build your own from the Sales Competency Self-Rating Scorecard rather than wait.

Ask two: real calls, graded against the map, not against gut feel. "That call sounded good" is not feedback — it's a mood. Feedback is: "In that discovery call, you asked four situation questions and one impact question. Proficient reps flip that ratio. You're novice-to-developing on questioning depth, proficient on rapport." That level of specificity requires your manager to actually listen to a recording against a rubric, not skim their memory of the call between two other meetings. Push for it weekly, not quarterly — waiting for a formal 30-60-90 checkpoint to hear this for the first time means you've spent 60 days practising the wrong habit at volume. If your manager balks at the time cost, offer to do the first pass yourself and bring it to them for a sanity check — most managers will grade your self-assessment far faster than they'll generate one from scratch.

Ask three: once you know your gaps, ask for a say in sequencing. The standard onboarding curriculum is built for an average new hire who doesn't exist. If your grading shows you're already developing-to-proficient on rapport but stuck at novice on objection reframing, sitting through three more weeks of rapport-building content because that's what week four says on the onboarding calendar is wasted time. Bring your gap to your manager and ask to swap in a targeted objection-handling stretch instead — most enablement calendars have more flex in them than reps assume, because nobody's asked before.

Why this compounds instead of just helping you hit quota faster

A rep who ramps by absorbing whatever the room does that week ends up with a grab-bag of habits copied from whoever they happened to shadow — some good, some idiosyncratic, most untested against an actual standard. A rep who ramps by mapping, measuring, and sequencing their own development ends up with something durable: a working model of their own skill profile that keeps paying out long after the 90-day mark, because they know how to keep doing it themselves. That's the difference between a rep who needs to be told what to work on for the next five years and one who can tell you. The second kind gets promoted faster, not because they closed more in month one, but because their manager trusts them to run their own development without hand-holding — which is, not coincidentally, most of what the next role up actually requires. Track it from day one with a proper Individual Development Plan (IDP) Template for Sales Reps and you'll have a paper trail that does the arguing for you at review time.

The map, the grading, and the say in sequencing cost your manager maybe two hours total across your first month. Most will never offer it unprompted. Almost none will refuse it if you ask specifically, on day three, before the pattern of passive ramp sets in and becomes the path of least resistance for both of you.
