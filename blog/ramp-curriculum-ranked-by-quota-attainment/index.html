The 90-Day Ramp Curriculum, Ranked by What Actually Predicts Quota Attainment

Most onboarding curricula run product training first and live calls last. What actually correlates with hitting quota says do the opposite.

Ask ten enablement leaders to sketch their new-hire curriculum and you'll get ten near-identical week-by-week grids: product certification first, CRM and battlecards second, a shadowing rotation third, one scripted role-play somewhere in the middle to tick the "practice" box, and: around week seven or eight, once the rep has survived several dozen hours of internal content, the first live call with an actual prospect. Everyone nods at this order because it feels responsible. Get the rep bulletproof on the product before you let them near a customer.

The problem is that "feels responsible" and "produces reps who hit quota faster" are not the same test. Rank each standard component of a 90-day curriculum by what it actually predicts about ramp speed, and the sequence most companies run is close to inverted. Here's the order that matches reality, not the order that matches a training calendar.

The ranking, most predictive to least

RankComponentWhy it sits here
1Live calls (real prospects, from week 2–3)The only rehearsal that matches the exam. Everything else is a proxy.
2Scripted role-play, graded against a rubricCompresses hundreds of reps into a fraction of the reps and hours, if someone actually scores it
3Structured shadowing with a debriefHigh ceiling, almost never executed with the debrief that makes it work
4CRM trainingHygiene, not skill. Predicts data quality, not quota attainment
5Competitor battlecardsReference material people mistake for readiness
6Product certification (as commonly run)The ritual that eats the whole calendar and correlates weakly with speed to first deal

1. Live calls: the only rehearsal that matches the exam

A rep's job is to have a conversation with a stranger who doesn't want to be sold to, under time pressure, with imperfect information. No amount of internal content simulates that, because the thing that actually separates ramped reps from unramped ones isn't knowledge: it's composure under a live, unscripted objection. A rep who's fielded eleven real "we're happy with our current provider" pushbacks has a different nervous system to one who's read the battlecard on it. I've watched reps who scored top of the class on product certification freeze solid on their first live call because nobody had told them the prospect wouldn't follow the happy-path script. The fix isn't more prep. It's earlier exposure to the real thing, at low stakes: cold or low-value accounts first, not the accounts that matter.

Measure this properly rather than trusting gut feel about who's "ready", the Sales Ramp-Time Calculator is worth running against your last four cohorts before you argue about sequencing at all. Most teams are surprised by which cohort actually ramped fastest, and it's rarely the one that got the most classroom hours.

2. Scripted role-play: but only if someone grades it

Role-play is the closest thing to live calls you can run before a rep is customer-facing, and it's the second-best predictor of ramp speed: with one condition almost nobody meets: somebody has to grade it against a rubric, out loud, with the rep watching the tape back. "Great job, really solid energy" is not feedback, it's a participation trophy. What predicts ramp speed is a manager saying "you asked a closed question at 0:47 where an open one would have surfaced the real objection, here's what that sounds like instead." Role-play without grading is theatre that happens to look like practice. Teams run it, tick the box, and wonder why it didn't transfer.

3. Structured shadowing: high value, badly executed almost everywhere

Done properly, shadowing is a strong predictor: the rep watches a skilled peer handle a real conversation, then someone walks them through why each move happened, not just what happened. Done as it's usually done: sit in on six calls, take some notes, nobody debriefs, it's closer to background noise. The value isn't in the watching. It's in someone naming the skill behind the moment afterwards. "Notice how she didn't answer the price question directly, she reframed to value first: that's a qualification move, not a stalling tactic." Without that sentence, shadowing teaches osmosis, and osmosis is slow. Run it with the debrief and it earns its slot near the top; run it without and it's closer to rank five. The Call Shadowing Checklist for New Reps exists specifically because most shadowing rotations skip the one step that makes it work.

4. CRM training: necessary hygiene, zero skill transfer

A rep who can't log an opportunity correctly will generate bad pipeline data, and that's a real cost. But CRM proficiency doesn't predict whether they can run a discovery call or handle a stall. It's operational competence, not sales skill, and treating it as part of the skill curriculum, rather than a separate onboarding checklist item, is exactly the kind of category error that pads week one with hours that don't build ramp readiness.

5. Competitor battlecards, reference material mistaken for readiness

Battlecards answer "what do I say when they mention Competitor X." They're genuinely useful in the moment, as a lookup. What they don't do is build the underlying skill of handling comparison objections in general, because the rep who's memorised six competitor rebuttals still has nothing when the prospect brings up a competitor that isn't on the card, or frames the comparison in a way the script didn't anticipate. Battlecards are a crutch that works until the exact scenario it was written for doesn't show up, which in a live call is more often than teams expect.

6. Product certification: the ritual that eats the whole calendar

This is the one that will get pushback, so let's be precise about the claim. Product knowledge matters. A rep who can't explain what the product does will lose credibility fast. But the standard execution of product certification: a multi-day marathon of feature walkthroughs and a multiple-choice quiz at the end: correlates weakly with how fast someone ramps, because passing a quiz on features doesn't test whether the rep can translate those features into a discovery conversation. It tests recall, not application. Worse, it's usually scheduled first and takes the longest, so it sets the tone: this job is about knowing things, not doing things. Reps arrive at their first live call having spent three weeks being tested on trivia and thirty minutes being tested on conversation.

Why the order gets built backwards

The standard sequence isn't accidental: it's optimised for manager comfort, not skill transfer. Product certification first because it's the safest thing to measure: a quiz score is defensible in a way "this rep handled three tough objections well" isn't. Live calls last because putting an unready-looking rep in front of a real prospect feels risky, and nobody wants to be the manager who let a green rep blow a deal. Battlecards and CRM training sit early because they're cheap to deliver and easy to check off a compliance list. Every part of the standard order reduces the manager's anxiety about what happens when a new hire is in the room with a customer. None of it is chosen because it's what the data on ramp speed says works.

What flipping it actually looks like

  1. Days 1–5: Lightweight product orientation (what it does, who it's for: not a certification exam), plus CRM basics. Enough to not embarrass themselves, no more.
  2. Days 3–10: Structured shadowing begins immediately, with a mandatory 15-minute debrief after every session naming the skill behind each moment.
  3. Days 7–14: Graded role-play starts, using real objections pulled from actual call recordings, not hypothetical scenarios written by someone in enablement.
  4. Days 12–21: First live calls, on low-stakes accounts, with a manager listening live or reviewing the recording within 24 hours.
  5. Days 20–45: Battlecards and deeper product certification layer in as reference material, introduced exactly when the rep hits the real-world scenario that makes them relevant, not before.
  6. Days 30–90: Live call volume ramps, role-play continues on the specific gaps the last week of calls exposed, not a generic curriculum.

The New Hire Sales Onboarding Checklist (Day 1 to Day 90) is built around this resequencing rather than the classroom-first default, if you want the full day-by-day version rather than the argument for it.

The objection you'll get

Someone will say: you can't put a rep on a live call in week two, they'll damage the brand or blow a real opportunity. Fair, and the answer isn't to delay live exposure until they're theoretically bulletproof: it's to control the stakes of the accounts they're calling, not the calendar date they start calling. A rep on a low-value renewal or a cold list in week two is lower risk than the same rep's first ever live call happening in week eight on a warmer, more consequential account, which is what usually happens instead. Delaying exposure doesn't reduce risk. It just moves the first mistake to a moment where it costs more.

The sequence most companies run wasn't designed by anyone. It accreted, one comfortable-sounding week-one slot at a time, until the curriculum optimised for looking thorough on a slide rather than producing reps who hit number. Rank the components honestly and the fix is uncomfortable but simple: whatever gets the rep talking to a real human fastest, with a real skill named after each attempt, goes first. Everything else is scaffolding around that, not a substitute for it.
