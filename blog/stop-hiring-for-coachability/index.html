Stop Hiring for 'Coachability.' It's a Weasel Word for 'I Liked Them.'

Every scorecard has a coachability box. Almost none define what would make someone fail it.

Every hiring scorecard I've ever been handed has a box marked "coachability," usually somewhere near the bottom, scored on the same 1-to-5 scale as everything else. Nobody has ever shown me the rubric for what a 2 looks like. Ask ten sales managers what would make a candidate fail that box and you'll get ten different, mostly circular answers: "they didn't seem open to feedback," "they were a bit defensive," "something about their energy." Strip out the vocabulary and what's left is: I didn't like them, and I've found a box on the scorecard that lets me write that down without saying it.

That's the actual function "coachability" serves in most sales hiring processes. Not a competency. A permission slip.

Why it's unfalsifiable

A criterion is only useful if there's a way to fail it that isn't just "I had a bad feeling." Ask yourself what actually gets scored under coachability in a typical interview, and it's almost always one of these:

  • Did they nod along when the interviewer described the team's process? (Compliance, not coachability.)
  • Did they say something humble about a past mistake? (Rehearsed, not observed.)
  • Did they seem likeable and low-friction to manage? (Personality, not skill.)

None of these involve giving the candidate anything to actually respond to. You can't test whether someone changes their approach under feedback if you never gave them any feedback. It's the equivalent of scoring someone's "resilience" by asking if they consider themselves resilient, and writing down whatever they say.

The cost of this isn't abstract. "Coachable" is doing a huge amount of quiet work in sales hiring, because managers know, correctly, that raw talent means nothing if a rep won't adjust their pitch after the fifth time you tell them it isn't landing. But if the box is unfalsifiable, you're not filtering for that. You're filtering for people who are good at performing agreeableness in a forty-five-minute conversation with a stranger: which correlates with almost nothing you actually need six months in, when the feedback is "your discovery calls are too shallow" and the response needs to be a changed behaviour, not a nod.

The fix: give them feedback and watch what happens

Coachability isn't a trait you can interview for by asking about it. It's a behaviour you can only observe by triggering it. So trigger it.

Run a live roleplay: a cold call or a discovery call, doesn't matter which, as long as it's specific enough to produce a real performance rather than a generic one. Let the candidate run it for three or four minutes uninterrupted. Then stop. Give one piece of specific, real feedback: not "good energy, keep going," an actual correction: "You asked four questions there and every one of them was answerable with a single word. Ask me something I'd need thirty seconds to answer." Then restart the roleplay from roughly the same point, and watch.

There are only three things that happen next, and all three are diagnostic:

  1. They visibly adjust. The next question is open-ended, built on what you just told them. This is the behaviour "coachable" is supposed to describe, and now you've actually seen it rather than been told about it.
  2. They agree verbally and then do the same thing anyway. This is worth naming precisely, because it's the pattern most interviewers miss entirely: they hear "you're right, totally" and score it as coachable, when the only evidence that matters is what happened in the very next question. Say-do gap, live, in the room.
  3. They get defensive or explain why the original approach was fine. This is the actual failure mode "coachability" is trying to catch, and it's now something you watched happen rather than a feeling you had about their tone.

Score against that, and the box stops being a permission slip. It becomes evidence: the same kind of evidence you'd want for any other competency on the framework, because that's what this is: a named, observable skill, not a personality trait.

Doing it properly

This only works if the feedback you give is real, specific, and about the actual thing they just did: generic feedback ("try to be more consultative") gives them nothing to visibly act on, and you'll misread confusion as resistance. The SBI Feedback Model Cheat Sheet is built for exactly this: Situation, Behaviour, Impact: and it forces you to give feedback specific enough that a candidate genuinely can respond to it, which is the whole point of the exercise. If you want the roleplay itself scripted so you're not improvising the prospect persona while also trying to watch for the adjustment, the Cold Call & Discovery Roleplay Interview Script gives you a structure to run it consistently across every candidate, so you're comparing like for like instead of grading whoever happened to get the easier version of you as the prospect.

It's worth saying what this costs you: it takes longer than ticking a box, and it requires you to actually deliver a piece of feedback well, under time pressure, to a stranger: which is its own skill, and no bad thing to get better at before you're doing it for real with someone on your team. But the alternative is what most sales orgs are already running: a scorecard with a coachability box on it, filled in by feel, defended by nobody, predicting nothing.

The candidates who pass this test aren't the ones who smile and say "absolutely, that's great feedback." They're the ones whose next sentence is visibly different from their last one. That's the only version of coachable that was ever worth hiring for.
