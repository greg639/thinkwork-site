Building a Multi-Threading Scorecard That Grades Depth, Not Headcount

Five contacts in the CRM tells you nothing about whether any of them would go to bat for you. A scorecard that grades buying-role coverage instead of counting names.

Five names in the CRM contact tab feels like coverage. It isn't. I've watched a "well-threaded" enterprise deal: five contacts logged, four different departments: go quiet for seven weeks the moment the champion took a job at a competitor, because not one of the other four had ever spoken to the rep without that champion in the room. The deal wasn't multi-threaded. It was single-threaded five times over, with one person doing the work of introducing everyone else.

Multi-threading became a metric because someone, correctly, noticed that single-threaded deals die when the one contact leaves, goes quiet, or gets overruled. The fix that got adopted, count the contacts, solves the wrong problem. It rewards a rep for CC'ing five people onto one call and calling it multi-threading. What actually predicts whether a deal survives procurement, a reorg, or a champion's LinkedIn status change is whether more than one of those people would independently vouch for you, push back internally on your behalf, or keep the deal moving if the primary contact vanished tomorrow.

A scorecard that grades headcount asks "how many." A scorecard that grades depth asks "would any of these people go to bat for me, and can I prove it." Here's how to build the second one.

Start with the buying roles you need, not the names you have

Before scoring anyone, map the roles a deal of this size and complexity typically requires:

  • Economic buyer: controls or signs off on budget
  • Champion: internally motivated to see this specific solution win, not just engaged with it
  • Technical evaluator: has veto power on capability or fit and will be asked to validate the choice
  • End-user representative: will actually use the product day to day and can kill adoption post-sale
  • Procurement, legal or security, gates the paperwork regardless of how sold everyone else is

Five roles. If you've got five contacts but three of them are end-users and nobody from procurement or the economic buyer's office has ever spoken to you, you don't have five-deep coverage. You have one role covered five times. Whitespace Mapping Worksheet is the tool for finding which roles nobody's touched yet.

Score depth per role, not presence per role

For every role you've identified a contact for, score it 0 to 3:

ScoreWhat it looks like
0Name only. No 1:1 contact, no email reply, exists in an org chart you found on LinkedIn.
1Has taken a call, but only ever in a group setting with your champion or AE present. Has never initiated contact themselves.
2Has taken a 1:1 call without other stakeholders in the room. Replies to messages within a business day without being chased.
3Has done something that cost them something: made an introduction unprompted, shared information that wasn't theirs to volunteer (internal timeline, competing vendor, budget ceiling), or defended the deal to a colleague when you weren't in the room.

A 0 or 1 on a role is not coverage. It's a name. Deals that survive a champion's departure or a procurement swap are the ones with at least two roles sitting at 2 or higher, because a contact scoring 2-plus has demonstrated they'll act without being managed into it.

Run the champion test before anyone gets called a champion

"Champion" gets handed to whoever's friendliest on the call. Test it with three questions the rep should be able to answer with a specific instance, not a feeling:

  1. Has this person introduced you to someone more senior without being asked twice?
  2. Have they shared something they didn't have to: a real budget number, a competitor's pricing, an internal deadline?
  3. Have they defended the deal to a skeptic on their own team, in a meeting you weren't in, and told you about it afterward?

Zero specific instances means you have an enthusiastic contact, not a champion. That distinction matters more at renewal than at close, because the enthusiastic contact goes quiet the moment things get hard, and the actual champion picks up the phone.

Put it on one page

Here's the scorecard applied to a mid-market deal with five logged contacts:

Five names on paper. On the scorecard: one real relationship, one workable one, and two roles either untouched or unstarted. That's the honest read of this deal's risk: not "5/5 threaded," which is what a CRM contact count tells a VP skimming a pipeline review.

Use it in the deal review, not just the CRM

The scorecard only earns its keep if it changes what happens in the room. In a Deal Review Checklist-style weekly review, the question stops being "how many contacts on this deal" and becomes "which roles are still at 0 or 1, and what's the specific next action to move one of them to a 2." That's coachable and assignable. "Get more people involved" isn't.

For accounts big enough to run over multiple quarters, fold the same role-and-depth grid into your Strategic Account Plan Template so it isn't a one-off exercise before a QBR: it's a standing view of where the account's actual risk sits, updated as roles move up or down rather than added to.

What changes when you switch metrics

Headcount metrics reward activity: book the call, log the contact, move on. Depth metrics reward the harder, slower work of getting a second person to act without being asked. The second thing survives a reorg. The first thing looks good in a pipeline review the week before the deal collapses.

If your current multi-threading metric is a contact count, you're not measuring multi-threading. You're measuring how many names a rep can get onto one Zoom call. Grade the four questions above instead, and you'll find out, months before procurement does, which deals only look covered.
