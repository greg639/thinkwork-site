What 'Sales Coaching' Actually Means, and Why Most of What's Called Coaching Isn't

Feedback, direction-giving, and cheerleading all get labeled 'coaching.' None of them are, unless a specific behavior gets named, diagnosed, practiced, and followed up on.

Ask a room of sales managers whether they coach their team and every hand goes up. Ask them to describe the last coaching conversation they had and most of them will describe something that isn't coaching at all: it's feedback, or direction-giving, or cheerleading wearing a coaching label, because "I gave Dana some coaching on that call" sounds better in a 1:1 with your own boss than "I told her what she did wrong" or "I told her what to say next time" or "I told her she's doing great."

None of those three things are bad, exactly. They're just not coaching, and the confusion costs teams a lot more than semantics: a manager who thinks feedback is coaching stops after step one of a four-step process, then wonders why the same mistake shows up on the call six weeks later.

The three things that get mislabelled

Feedback is telling someone what happened. "You went straight to price when they raised the budget objection, you didn't ask what was driving it." True, specific, a useful first move. But feedback alone doesn't touch why it happened, so the rep hears it, agrees, and does the same thing next time under the same pressure, because nobody found the actual gap underneath the behaviour.

Direction-giving is telling someone what to do instead. "Next time, ask what's making the budget the sticking point before you offer anything." This feels like coaching because it's specific and actionable, and managers love it because it produces a visible change fast, the rep says the line next week. But it's a script patch, not a skill build. The moment the conversation deviates from the exact scenario the manager anticipated, the rep has nothing, because they were handed an answer, not a capability.

Cheerleading is encouragement detached from either. "Great energy on that call, keep it up." It feels supportive, costs nothing, and changes nothing, because it doesn't name a behaviour at all: it's a mood, not a diagnosis.

All three get called "coaching" in performance reviews, 1:1 notes, and manager training decks. None of them, alone, produce skill development, and you can prove it with a simple test: pull three coaching notes from your own CRM or 1:1 doc and ask whether the same behaviour shows up again in the rep's next five calls. If it does, whatever you did wasn't coaching: it was one of the three things above, dressed up.

What coaching actually requires

Real coaching is a closed loop with four steps, and skipping any one of them breaks the loop:

StepWhat it meansWhat breaks if you skip it
1. Observe a specific behaviourNot "the call went okay," but the exact moment: what was said, what wasn't asked, what the prospect did in responseYou're coaching from memory or impression, and impressions are unreliable within about 48 hours
2. Diagnose the underlying skill gapWhy the behaviour happened: a discovery-questioning gap, a confidence gap under pushback, a product-knowledge gapYou fix the symptom (this one call) instead of the cause (every call like it)
3. Prescribe a practice repA drill on that exact gap, run live, before the next real callThe rep intellectually agrees and behaviourally doesn't change, because insight isn't the same as capability
4. Follow up on the same behaviourCheck the next call for the same moment, not a general "how's it going"You never know if the loop actually closed, and neither does the rep

Notice that steps one and two are diagnostic: observe, then diagnose, before step three ever prescribes anything. That ordering isn't a nicety. A manager who prescribes before diagnosing is doing direction-giving with extra steps: they've decided what the rep should do without confirming what's actually wrong.

A worked example

A rep's discovery calls are converting to demos at half the team average. Feedback-only version: "Your discovery calls feel rushed, slow down." True, unhelpful, unfollowed. Direction-giving version: "Ask five open questions before you pitch anything." Better, still a patch. Coaching version: the manager reviews three calls against a Discovery Call Scorecard and notices the rep asks plenty of questions but never follows up when the prospect gives a vague answer: the gap isn't questioning volume, it's questioning depth. The prescribed practice rep is a live drill: the manager plays a prospect who gives deliberately vague answers, and the rep has to dig twice before moving on. Follow-up: the manager reviews the rep's next two discovery calls specifically for follow-up questions after vague answers, using the SBI Feedback Model Cheat Sheet to keep the debrief anchored to that one behaviour instead of drifting into a general performance chat.

That's four steps, in order, on one named skill. It took longer to set up than "slow down" did. It's also the only version of the three that has any real chance of showing up differently in month two.

Why the label actually matters

Calling feedback, direction-giving, and cheerleading "coaching" isn't just imprecise, it lets managers believe they've done something they haven't. A manager who logs twenty "coaching conversations" a month and can't point to a single skill gap that closed as a result isn't under-coaching by volume; they're coaching zero times and doing something else twenty times. If you want to find out which one you're actually doing, run your own coaching notes against a Coaching Effectiveness Scorecard for Managers and see how many of the four steps show up per rep, per month. Most managers are surprised by how often they stop at step one.

The word "coaching" got generous somewhere along the way, generous enough to cover almost any conversation with a rep that isn't purely administrative. Tighten the definition back up and the job gets harder to fake and easier to actually do.
