We Asked Sales Leaders How They Know Their Managers Can Actually Coach. Most Didn't Have an Answer.

'They've been doing it ten years' is not a measurement. Composite notes from conversations with revenue leaders on the question most of them had never been asked directly.

Ask a VP of Sales how they know their frontline managers can actually coach, and you get one of three answers almost every time: "they've been doing it a long time," "the reps seem to like them," or a pause. None of those three is a measurement. Over a run of conversations with revenue leaders this year: some running twenty-person teams, some running two hundred: I started asking this question on purpose, partly to see how people who are otherwise ruthless about pipeline math and forecast accuracy react to being asked for the same rigour on coaching. Most hadn't been asked directly before. A few, once pressed past the first answer, admitted they genuinely didn't know.

What follows is composite: patterns pulled across a number of these conversations, not a transcript of any one of them, because the pattern mattered more than any individual leader's specific answer.

The tenure trap

The most common first answer was tenure. A CRO at a mid-market SaaS business put it plainly: "Dave's been managing that team for six years, he knows what he's doing." Pressed on what "knows what he's doing" was actually based on, the answer moved to retention and quota attainment for Dave's team: both real signals, both also explainable by inherited pipeline quality, territory assignment, and market conditions that have nothing to do with Dave's coaching.

Tenure measures survival, not skill. A manager can run the same year of coaching ten times in a row: same diagnoses, same fixes, same blind spots, and tenure alone can't tell you whether year six looks any different from year one. Several leaders, once they'd said this out loud themselves, visibly didn't love hearing it.

The rep-liking trap

The second most common answer was some version of "the reps like working for them." This one is more dangerous than tenure because it feels like real signal: engaged reps, low attrition, good survey scores. But being liked and being diagnostically sharp are only loosely correlated, and the correlation runs in an uncomfortable direction at the tail ends: the manager willing to tell a rep the specific, uncomfortable, correctly-diagnosed truth about their skill gap is not always the most popular manager in the building. A VP of Sales at a 150-person fintech offered the sharpest version of this: "My best-liked manager and my best-coaching manager are not the same person, and it took me two years to admit that to myself."

Three questions that expose the gap

None of this is an argument for ignoring tenure or likeability, they're just not measurement. Three questions, asked in this order, get to the real answer faster than either proxy above, and each one is harder to fake than the last.

  1. "Pick a rep on your team who's stuck. What's the one skill holding them back: not the deal, the skill?" This is the fastest exposer because it demands specificity on the spot. Almost every leader can answer for the deal: "we lost it on price," "the champion went dark." Ask for the underlying skill instead and the answer either comes immediately and precisely, or it doesn't come at all. There's no comfortable middle ground, which is exactly why it goes first.
  2. "Show me the last coaching plan you built for someone, not the compliment you gave them." This one is slower to answer because it demands an artefact, not a memory. A real coaching plan names the competency, the practice path, and a check-in date. A lot of "coaching" turns out to be warm conversation with no document behind it at all: which isn't nothing, but it isn't a plan, and it's easy to confuse the two until someone asks you to produce one.
  3. "When did your manager last change their mind about what's wrong with a rep, based on new evidence rather than gut?" This is the hardest question and it goes last because it requires the leader to think longitudinally about someone else's thinking, not just recall a single instance. A manager who's actually diagnosing will occasionally be wrong and revise. A manager running on instinct forms an opinion of a rep in month one and coaches to that opinion for the next two years regardless of what changes. Most leaders need real time to answer this one honestly, and several came back to it days later with a different answer than the one they gave in the room.

What good measurement looks like instead

The leaders who had a real answer to the opening question: a genuine minority: all had one thing in common: they'd stopped relying on gut impressions of their managers and started using something structured to check diagnostic quality directly, rather than inferring it from team results. If you don't have that yet, a Sales Leadership Competency Scorecard gives you a starting structure for what to actually look for, and a Call Coaching Feedback Template gives your managers: and you, reviewing them, a consistent artefact to check rather than a memory of how the conversation felt.

The honest ones in this set of conversations weren't the leaders with the best answer. They were the ones willing to sit with the pause instead of filling it with tenure or likeability. That pause is the most useful thirty seconds most sales leaders spend all quarter, if they let it run long enough to notice they don't actually know.
