The Discovery Skill That's Actually Gating Your Best AEs From Becoming Managers

The AEs who get promoted aren't the best closers. They're the ones who can diagnose a deal's real qualification state out loud, for someone else, in real time.

Every VP of Sales has, at some point, promoted the wrong person for the right reason. The logic runs: this rep carries the biggest number, so they'll carry the team. Eighteen months later that AE is either quietly asking to go back into an individual contributor seat, or quietly ruining four other reps' careers, and nobody in the room who made the call can explain what went wrong, because on paper the numbers said yes.

I've made this mistake myself, more than once, across three CCO seats. The number on the board tells you almost nothing about whether someone can run a team. What it tells you is that they can run a deal.

The skill nobody tests for

Closing and discovery execution are individual-contributor skills. They're about one person doing a thing well, for their own pipeline, under their own name. Management is a second-order skill: the ability to watch somebody else do the thing, work out precisely where it broke, and say so in a way that changes their next attempt. That's not the same muscle. Plenty of brilliant surgeons make lousy professors of surgery.

The specific version of this that actually predicts management readiness in sales, in my experience building competency frameworks across dozens of sales organisations, is narrated qualification diagnosis. Can this person sit in on somebody else's discovery call — cold, no prep, no deal history — and tell you, in real time or straight after, exactly where the qualification broke down and why?

Not "he should have asked about budget." Anyone can say that. I mean something like: "He got a stated pain in the first four minutes, but he never got the buyer to put a number on what that pain costs monthly, so when the champion goes to fight for budget internally next week, she's got nothing to fight with. That's why this deal sits in proposal for six weeks and dies quietly." That's a diagnosis. It names the specific qualification gap, traces the causal line to the outcome, and does it fast enough to be useful in a coaching conversation that's actually happening — not a post-mortem three months later.

Why your best closers so often can't do this

The uncomfortable finding, from years of running assessment against a proper competency taxonomy rather than gut feel, is that strong individual closers are frequently the worst at this. They close well because their qualification instincts have gone unconscious — compiled, in the programming sense. They can't tell you why they asked the question they asked, because they stopped consciously deciding years ago. Ask a rep running at 130% of quota to explain what a colleague did wrong on a call and you often get vibes: "yeah, that felt a bit off," "I don't know, I think he could've pushed harder." Correct instinct, zero diagnostic language. That rep is superb in a deal and would be useless in a 1:1 coaching session, because coaching means making the unconscious conscious, on someone else's call, under time pressure.

The AEs who turn out to be strong managers are usually a tier below the very top closer, and they're the ones who, unprompted, are already narrating what they see — in deal reviews, in Slack threads about a colleague's stuck opportunity, in the way they debrief their own losses. They're doing the management skill before anyone hands them the title.

What actually predicts readiness

Here's how I'd rank the signals I look for when deciding who's ready to run people, most predictive first, with the reasoning for each.

RankSignalWhy it predicts management readiness
1Unprompted, specific diagnosis of a peer's qualification gap, with a causal line to the deal outcomeThis is the actual job. Everything else is downstream of it
2Can articulate their own qualification process step by step, not just execute itIf they can't explain their own instincts, they can't transfer them to someone else
3Seeks out other people's calls voluntarily, without being askedShows the coaching orientation exists before the title does
4Adjusts diagnostic language to the person, not just the dealDistinguishes a coach from a critic — same gap, different rep, different delivery
5Win rate / quota attainmentTells you they can do the job themselves. Tells you almost nothing about whether they can get twelve other people to do it

Quota attainment sits last, deliberately. Not irrelevant — a manager with no credibility from having carried a number will struggle to get a room's attention — but it's a threshold requirement, not a differentiator. Once someone has cleared "credible enough to have earned the room," everything above it is what actually separates a good manager from a bad one.

How to build it before you need it

Nobody develops this on purpose, which is the real problem. Organisations wait until promotion day and then wonder why the new manager's 1:1s consist of "good call" and "needs to ask more questions." If you want your best AEs ready before you need them to be, the fix is structural, not aspirational.

  1. Put your promotion candidates into call reviews on other reps' calls — not their own — months before you're seriously considering them for the role. A shared Discovery Call Scorecard gives them a vocabulary to diagnose against, rather than vibes, which is where most untrained feedback lands.
  2. Make them write the feedback down before they say it out loud. A Call Coaching Feedback Template forces the causal chain — gap, why it matters, what to do differently — instead of a single adjective.
  3. Track it as its own line in your progression conversations, separate from quota. If your internal levelling only measures attainment, you're building your management bench on the one variable that doesn't predict management success. A Sales Career Level Matrix (IC1 → Principal/Enterprise) that names diagnostic coaching as its own competency, rather than folding it into vague "leadership potential," makes it something people can actually practise toward.

The part that's uncomfortable to say out loud

The rep who is best at the job and the rep who is best at explaining the job to somebody else are, on the evidence, often not the same person. Most sales organisations have already noticed this, quietly, in the amount of regret that follows a certain kind of promotion. Few have built a process that tests for the actual predictive skill directly, because it's easier to promote the number and hope the rest arrives with the title.

It doesn't arrive with the title. It either exists already, unrewarded and unnamed, in the AE who's been informally coaching their deskmate for a year without anyone noticing — or it gets built, slowly, by putting people in front of other people's calls long before you ever hand them a team.
