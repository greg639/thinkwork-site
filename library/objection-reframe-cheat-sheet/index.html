Objection Reframe Cheat Sheet

Sandler-style reversals for the 20 objections reps hear most — every one answered with a question that reopens the conversation instead of a rebuttal that shuts it down.

What's inside

  • 20 of the most common sales objections, verbatim as buyers actually say them
  • A word-for-word reversal question for each one, Sandler-style (answer a question with a question)
  • A one-line note on what each reversal is designed to surface
  • Grouped by objection type: timing/stall, budget, competitor loyalty, authority, and price
  • A short explainer on why reversals outperform rebuttals
  • Ground rules for tone so the reversal reads as curious, not confrontational

The Sandler principle behind every line here: never answer an objection directly — reverse it back with a question that makes the prospect do the work of explaining themselves. A rebuttal invites a debate. A reversal invites a real answer, and real answers are what let you qualify or disqualify fast.

Say these in your own natural voice. The structure matters more than the exact wording.

1. "We're not ready to buy right now." → "Not ready is fair. Is it the timing that's wrong, or is it that you're not convinced this solves the right problem yet?" (Splits a real stall from a soft no.)

2. "Send me some information." → "Happy to. When you say 'information,' what specifically are you hoping it answers?" (Turns a brush-off into a real requirement you can respond to.)

3. "We don't have budget." → "Good to know. Is there no budget for this at all, or no budget for it yet, this quarter?" (Distinguishes a hard no from a timing/sequencing issue.)

4. "We're happy with our current vendor." → "That's good to hear. What would have to change about them for 'happy' to become 'not happy'?" (Surfaces the switching threshold instead of arguing against a vendor you can't see.)

5. "I need to think about it." → "Of course. What specifically do you need to think through — the fit, the price, or something else?" (Names the real blocker instead of letting "think about it" become silence.)

6. "Your price is too high." → "Too high compared to what? Help me understand what you're comparing it against." (Exposes whether the anchor is a competitor, an internal budget, or nothing at all.)

7. "We need to check with legal/procurement." → "Makes sense. What does legal/procurement typically need from us to move quickly — can we get that started now instead of after you decide?" (Moves paper process into parallel instead of sequential.)

8. "I'm not the decision maker." → "Understood. Who else, along with you, would need to agree, and what matters most to them?" (Maps the real committee instead of stalling on one person.)

9. "Call me back next quarter." → "I can do that. What changes between now and then that makes next quarter a better time than now?" (Tests whether there's a real trigger event or just avoidance.)

10. "We tried something like this before and it didn't work." → "That's important context. What was different about that attempt versus what we're discussing now?" (Surfaces the real failure cause instead of letting history kill the deal by association.)

11. "We're doing this in-house." → "That's a real option. What's the in-house build costing you in time and opportunity that you're not accounting for yet?" (Reframes build-vs-buy as a cost question, not a capability debate.)

12. "I don't see the ROI." → "Fair. Walk me through the numbers you'd need to see to be confident in it." (Hands the burden of defining proof back to the person who has to be convinced.)

13. "This isn't a priority right now." → "Understood — what is the priority right now, and what would have to happen for this to move up the list?" (Finds out if you're actually competing for attention, or just not urgent at all.)

14. "We're locked into a contract." → "Good to know. When does that come up for renewal, and would it help to have options on the table before that date?" (Converts a hard stop into a timeline to plan around.)

15. "I need to loop in more stakeholders." → "Absolutely — who else should be in the room, and what would they need to hear to get on board?" (Turns a delay tactic into a real committee-mapping question.)

16. "Can you just send a proposal?" → "I can — but a proposal without knowing a few things first would just be a guess dressed up as a number. Can we cover those quickly?" (Protects you from pricing yourself out with incomplete information.)

17. "We're too small/big for this." → "What makes you say that — is it about scale, or something specific about how this typically gets used?" (Tests whether it's a real disqualifier or an assumption from a different product category.)

18. "How is this different from [Competitor]?" → "Great question — what have you seen from them so far that's caught your attention?" (Lets you respond to their actual comparison instead of guessing at it.)

19. "I don't have time for a call." → "Totally get it. If I could get what you need in 10 minutes instead of 30, would that change things?" (Removes the time objection instead of arguing against it.)

20. "Just email me the pricing." → "I will — pricing depends on a couple of things though. Can I ask two quick questions first, so the number I send you is actually the right one?" (Keeps a conversation instead of becoming a vending machine for a number with no context.)

Ground rules for using these

  • Tone is everything. Every line above should sound genuinely curious, said with a slight upward inflection — not like a lawyer's cross-examination. If it sounds like a "gotcha," you're doing it wrong.
  • Silence after you ask is the point. Ask the reversal, then stop talking. The next person to speak loses control of the frame — let it be them.
  • Never stack two reversals in a row. If they answer your reversal with another objection, that second one gets a real, direct response — reversing twice in a row reads as evasive.
  • These qualify, they don't manipulate. If the honest answer is "no, this really isn't a fit," a good reversal should surface that just as fast as it surfaces a real yes.

How to use it

Keep this open on a second screen (or printed by your desk) during live calls; when you hear one of the 20 objections, respond with the matching reversal question verbatim, then stop talking and let the prospect fill the silence.
