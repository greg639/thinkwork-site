The Sales Leadership Manifesto Every VP Writes, and What It Always Leaves Out

'Servant leadership.' 'Radical candor.' 'Psychological safety.' A line-by-line read of the genre of leadership-philosophy posts that sales leaders write, and the one thing almost none of them specify: what a manager should actually watch for in a live call.

You can predict the post before the first sentence loads. A VP of Sales, somewhere between the honeymoon and the exit, sits down to explain what they believe about leading people. Eight hundred words later you know which management book they've just finished, you know they've had a hard year, and you still don't know what they'd tell a manager to actually watch for the next time a rep is on a call. That gap isn't an accident. It's the whole genre.

I've read hundreds of these. I've been asked to comment on dozens. For a while I wrote a version myself, because that's what you do at a certain altitude. What follows is not a takedown of any one person — it's a teardown of the format, because the format is the problem. It launders vocabulary as if vocabulary were a plan.

The furniture never changes

Strip the branding and every one of these posts has the same load-bearing walls. A cold open about a mistake early in the writer's career. A quoted line from a book — Brené Brown, Kim Scott, sometimes Patrick Lencioni if they're feeling structural. Three bullet points titled "what I believe," each one a noun phrase with no verb attached. A closing line inviting the reader to share their own philosophy in the comments, which nobody ever operationalises, including the person who asked.

What's missing is any sentence that starts with "so when a manager sits in on Thursday's call, they should—". That sentence never arrives. The philosophy sits one full altitude above the manager's actual Tuesday, and it stays there by design, because coming down a level means being specific enough to be wrong.

The five phrases doing the least work

Some phrases in this genre cost the writer more to use honestly than others. Ranked from cheapest — meaning least likely to ever be checked against a real behaviour — to least cheap:

RankPhraseWhy it sits here
1"I hire for coachability"Unfalsifiable before the fact and unexamined after it. Nobody audits which hires actually improved fastest against which skill, so the claim never has to survive contact with data.
2"Servant leadership"Borrowed wholesale from Greenleaf, decades old, applied to sales orgs with zero adaptation. It describes a posture, never a Tuesday-morning action, so it can't be contradicted by anything the writer actually does.
3"Radical candour"Cites the book, gestures at "caring personally, challenging directly," but almost never specifies what candid feedback on a named skill sounds like. Candour about what, exactly? The phrase does the emotional work and skips the content.
4"Psychological safety"Real construct, real research behind it — and almost always deployed here as an atmosphere rather than a structure. Nobody follows it with the operational question: safety to say what, to whom, about which mistake, with what consequence removed?
5"People over pipeline"The least cheap of the five because it at least implies a trade-off — but it's still a slogan, not a mechanism. No post that uses this line has ever shown me the mechanism that stops a forecast call from eating the 1:1 anyway.

Notice what they share: every one of them could be printed on a mug, and none of them tells a manager what to do differently in the next thirty minutes they spend with a rep.

What the philosophy is actually hiding

Here's a representative line, close to verbatim from three different posts I've read this year: "We coach the person, not the pipeline." Nice sentence. Now apply it. Priya's discovery calls are technically fluent and emotionally flat — she asks every question on the framework and the prospect still won't open up. What does the manager do on Wednesday?

The manifesto has nothing. It was never built to answer that question, because answering it requires naming a skill — rapport-building under structured questioning, say — describing what "better" looks like at the next level up, and prescribing something to practise before Priya's next live call. That's three concrete commitments. The manifesto offers zero, because the manifesto's job was to make the writer look reflective, not to make the manager's next hour better.

This is the tell across the whole genre: the vaguer the claim, the safer it is to publish, and the safer it is to publish, the further it drifts from anything a manager could be held to.

The question none of them answer

If you want to know whether a leadership philosophy is real or decorative, there's one test. Ask the person who wrote it: what should a manager be watching for in a rep's next call, and what do they do about it?

A usable answer has three parts, every time:

  1. The named skill — not "communication," but something specific enough to observe: negotiation opening, multi-threading into procurement, discovery questioning that goes past the first answer.
  2. The observable marker — what the manager is actually watching for on the call itself. Is the rep holding the number through the first counter, or folding at the first "that's a bit steep"? Is the rep asking a second question after the prospect's first answer, or moving straight to the next item on the script?
  3. The manager's next move — a specific rep, role-play, or shadowed call before the skill shows up live again, not a general instruction to "keep working on it."

A Discovery Call Scorecard gives you the second part in writing, so "watching for" isn't a vibe. The SBI Feedback Model Cheat Sheet gives you a structure for delivering what you saw without it curdling into either niceness or a lecture. And a Call Coaching Feedback Template closes the loop so the observation actually turns into a scheduled next action, not a nod in the debrief.

None of that is glamorous enough for a manifesto post. It doesn't quote a bestseller. It just happens to be the only version of "leadership philosophy" that a rep would notice the difference from.

Write the philosophy if it helps you think. Just don't confuse it with a coaching system, and don't let a VP two levels up mistake the mug-worthy line for evidence that the coaching is actually happening underneath it.
