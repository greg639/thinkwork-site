Ask a Skeptic: We Bought Four Content Tools and Reps Still Slack Their Manager for the Deck

A reader wrote in after rolling out four separate content tools, only to keep catching reps DMing their manager for 'the deck.' The tools weren't the problem.

A reader who runs enablement at a 90-person SaaS company wrote in with a version of a complaint I hear roughly once a quarter, usually from someone who's just finished a rollout and is trying to work out why it didn't take:

"We now own four content tools: a DAM for assets, a sales engagement platform with its own content library, a wiki for process docs, and a dedicated proposal tool. Between them we have more content than we've ever had. And reps still Slack their manager asking 'can you send me the deck.' What did we actually buy?"

Four tools, more content than ever, and the load-bearing system for finding anything is still a manager's DMs. That's not a tooling problem. It's what happens every time you solve "we need more content infrastructure" without ever answering the much smaller, much more boring question underneath it: for any given piece of content, which tool is the one authoritative place to find it, and who decided that.

The diagnostic question nobody asked before buying tool three

Here's the test I'd have run before signing off on the DAM, or the wiki, or any of it: pick ten pieces of content a rep needs in an average week: the current proposal template, the objection response for the pricing conversation, the onboarding checklist for a new logo, last quarter's win-loss summary: and for each one, ask "if a rep needs this at 4pm before a call, where do they go first, and is that the same answer for every rep on the floor?"

Most companies that own four tools have never run this test, because the tools were bought to solve four separate, real problems: a genuine DAM need, a genuine engagement-platform need, and each purchase made sense in isolation. Nobody was in the room for all four decisions asking the cross-cutting question, because it's not really a tooling question. It's a governance question, and governance doesn't show up on anyone's software shortlist.

Why Slack wins every time, structurally

Slacking the manager isn't laziness. It's the fastest, lowest-risk path to a correct answer, and reps are rational. Four tools means four places a document could plausibly live, no stated rule for which one is authoritative for which content type, and a real cost to guessing wrong, a rep who pulls the outdated proposal template from the wiki because that's where it lived eighteen months ago looks worse in front of a client than a rep who just asked.

The manager, meanwhile, has become the de facto single source of truth by accident, not by design. They know which tool has the current version because they've been burned before and remember. That knowledge lives in one person's head and a thread of DMs, which means it doesn't scale past the reps who already have a relationship with that manager, doesn't survive the manager going on leave, and generates zero data on what's actually being requested: because a Slack DM isn't measured, and a search inside your fourth content tool is.

What ownership actually needs to look like

The fix isn't a fifth tool, and it isn't a memo telling reps to "use the DAM." It's assigning, content type by content type, exactly one authoritative home and one accountable owner: written down somewhere reps can find it, not implied by whichever tool happened to get budget first.

Content typeAuthoritative toolOwner accountable for currency
Client-facing proposalsProposal toolDeal desk / enablement lead
Objection responses, talk tracksSales engagement platform libraryEnablement, reviewed quarterly with top reps
Onboarding and process docsWikiRevOps, reviewed at each process change
Brand assets, logos, case studiesDAMMarketing, on request from enablement

Four rows, four owners, no ambiguity: and critically, this table itself needs to live somewhere reps can actually find it (pinned in the sales engagement platform's homepage, not buried in an onboarding deck they saw once). The moment a rep can answer "which tool for this" without asking anyone, the Slack-the-manager pattern starts to break, because the DM stops being the fastest path to the answer.

The harder admission

The honest reason this doesn't get fixed after the fact is that assigning ownership means someone has to say no to a tool they already paid for being the answer for a given content type, and that's an uncomfortable conversation to have with whichever exec sponsored the DAM purchase. It's far easier to add a fifth tool that promises to unify the other four than to sit down and adjudicate who owns what in the four you have. I've watched this exact move happen: a "content hub" bought specifically to sit on top of the sprawl: and it solves nothing, because the underlying failure was never about search. It was about accountability, and a fifth tool doesn't create any.

Before buying anything else, it's worth being honest about how mature the underlying content operation actually is rather than assuming the next purchase will fix what governance should have. The RevOps Maturity Self-Assessment is a reasonable gut-check for that: most teams that own four overlapping tools score lower on ownership and governance than they expect to, which is usually the tell that the next fix is a decision, not a purchase.

And if you want to know whether the content you already have is even being used once ownership is sorted: rather than just assuming it will be, now that it's "findable", run it through the Sales Content Effectiveness Calculator. Findable and used are different things, and the second one is the only number that should change anyone's mind about tool five.

What I'd tell the reader directly

Don't buy anything else this quarter. Take the four tools you have, run the ten-piece-of-content test above, write down one owner per content type, and put that list somewhere more visible than a wiki page nobody bookmarks. The tools were never the gap. The gap was that nobody was willing to be the boring adult in the room and decide, in writing, whose job it is to keep each one current: and until that happens, the manager's DMs will keep winning, because right now they're the only system in the building anyone actually trusts.
