LAER Objection Resolution Framework Guide

A full teaching guide to the LAER framework (Listen-Acknowledge-Explore-Respond): the de-escalation sequence built for emotionally charged objections rooted in past bad experiences or distrust, with scripts for every step and a worked example.

How to use it

Walk through L-A-E-R in strict order on any emotionally charged objection, never skip Explore straight to Respond, and use the decision table to choose LAER over Feel-Felt-Found when the objection carries visible frustration or references a past bad experience.

Listen · Acknowledge · Explore · Respond

LAER is built for objections with emotional weight: frustration from a past bad experience, defensiveness about change, skepticism from being burned by a vendor before. Where Feel-Felt-Found is a persuasion technique, LAER is a de-escalation sequence: it lowers the emotional temperature before any selling happens at all.

Use LAER when: the prospect's tone shifts (sharper, faster, quieter), the objection references a past bad experience, or the objection is really about trust/change rather than logic. Use Feel-Felt-Found when: the objection is a straightforward, low-heat comparison ("your competitor is cheaper") and you have a clean, relevant story to tell.

L: Listen (fully, without formulating your response)

What it is: Give complete, visible attention until the prospect is completely finished: no interrupting, no "mm-hmm, but," no mentally drafting your rebuttal while they're still talking.

How to do it:

  • Stop moving toward your next point. Physically pause.
  • Let a silence of 1–2 seconds sit after they finish before you speak.
  • If it's a long objection, resist the urge to jump in at the first pause for breath, those aren't full stops.

Signal you're doing it right: you could repeat back specific words they used, not just the gist.

Signal you're doing it wrong: your first sentence back starts with "but" or "actually."

A: Acknowledge (validate the emotion, not just the content)

What it is: Name what you heard, including the feeling underneath it, before touching the substance.

Script examples:

  • "That sounds frustrating, especially if you'd already been burned by something similar before."
  • "I can hear that trust is the real issue here, not the feature set. That's fair."
  • "If I'd been through what you just described, I'd be skeptical of anyone walking in and pitching me too."

Do:

  • Name the emotion out loud if it's clearly present (frustration, skepticism, fatigue).
  • Keep it short, one or two sentences.

Don't:

  • Don't follow the acknowledgment with "but", it cancels the validation instantly.
  • Don't over-apologize on behalf of a competitor or your own company if you don't know the details yet: validate the feeling, not unverified facts.

E: Explore (ask before you explain)

What it is: Once the emotional temperature has come down a notch, ask questions to understand the specific shape of the concern, LAER's diagnostic step.

Script examples:

  • "What happened last time, if you don't mind me asking? I want to make sure we don't repeat it."
  • "When you say it never actually got adopted: was that a tool problem, a rollout problem, or something else?"
  • "What would need to be true this time for it to feel different?"

Why this step matters: in emotionally charged objections, the stated concern ("your tool won't work either") is almost always standing in for a more specific fear ("I'll look bad for recommending this again"). Explore is where that surfaces.

R: Respond (specific, proportionate, and tied to what you just heard)

What it is: Answer the specific thing uncovered in Explore: not a generic pitch, and not over-explaining.

Script examples:

  • "Given what happened with the rollout last time, here's specifically how we handle onboarding differently: [concrete mechanism], and I can put you directly in touch with a customer who had almost the identical experience before switching."
  • "That makes sense: given that, would it help if the first phase was scoped small enough that a repeat failure would cost you almost nothing to find out?"

Close the loop: always end Respond with a check: "Does that address what happened last time, or is there more underneath it?"

Full Worked Example

Prospect: "Honestly, we tried something like this two years ago and it was a disaster. I don't really want to go through that again."

Rep (Listen): [lets them finish, doesn't jump in]

Rep (Acknowledge): "That sounds like it was a genuinely bad experience, and I get why that makes you cautious about anything that sounds similar."

Rep (Explore): "Do you mind me asking what specifically went wrong: was it the tool itself, how it was rolled out, or something else?"

Prospect: "It was rolled out to the whole team at once with basically no training, and it just died."

Rep (Respond): "That's really useful to know, that's actually the opposite of how we'd approach it. We'd start with one team, with hands-on onboarding, before anyone else touches it. Would a small, contained pilot like that address the concern, or is there more to it?"

Common Mistakes When Using LAER

  1. Skipping straight to Respond, the most common failure. If you haven't Explored, your Response is a guess.
  2. Acknowledging and then arguing: "I hear you, but actually our tool is different" undoes the entire Acknowledge step.
  3. Treating Explore as an interrogation, one good open question beats four rapid-fire ones.
  4. Forgetting to close the loop, Respond without a check-back leaves you guessing whether it landed.

LAER vs. Feel-Felt-Found, Quick Decision Guide

SituationUse
Prospect references a past bad experience or is visibly frustratedLAER
Prospect is calm and comparing you to a competitor on price/featuresFeel-Felt-Found
You don't yet know why they're objectingLAER, Explore step will tell you
