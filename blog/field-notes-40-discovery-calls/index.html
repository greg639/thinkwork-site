Field Notes From Sitting In on 40 Discovery Calls in One Quarter

What actually happens on discovery calls when nobody's grading against a checklist, the patterns that don't show up in any framework.

Forty discovery calls, one quarter, no scorecard running while I listened: just a notebook and the transcript queue from Peer's assessment pipeline, which meant I could go back and check whether what I thought I'd heard was actually what got said. That distinction matters more than it sounds. Most of what people believe about discovery calls comes from the four or five they remember, usually the ones that closed. What follows isn't a methodology. It's what actually kept happening, across industries, reps, and deal sizes, that no acronym I know has a box for.

The buyer who qualifies themselves before you ask

By call six I'd stopped being surprised by this: a meaningful chunk of buyers, at least eleven of the forty did it clearly, volunteer their own qualification before the rep gets to ask for it. Not "we have budget," but the full shape of it: "look, I'll be straight with you, I've got sign-off up to £40k without finance, my boss cares about implementation time more than price, and I need this live before our renewal in March." Unprompted. Inside the first six minutes.

No discovery framework accounts for this properly, because every framework assumes the rep is extracting information from a reluctant or neutral party. What these calls actually needed was a rep who could recognise they'd just been handed the whole qualification and stop running their script. Four of the eleven reps kept going anyway: worked through their BANT list as written, re-asked the budget question the buyer had already answered unprompted three minutes earlier. The buyer's tone visibly shifted on the recording. Not hostile. Just recalibrating, downward, on whether this rep was actually listening.

The silence nobody can survive

Nineteen of forty calls had at least one moment where the buyer paused for more than four seconds after a question, and in seventeen of those the rep filled it. Every time, without exception, what the rep filled it with was worse than whatever the buyer had been about to say. Usually a rephrase of the question. Sometimes a multiple-choice version: "is it more of a timing thing, or a budget thing, or..." Occasionally an outright answer supplied on the buyer's behalf.

The two calls where the rep let it sit produced the two best answers in the entire sample. In one, a full ninety seconds of dead air before the buyer said, unprompted, that the real blocker wasn't anything on the rep's list, it was an unrelated reorg that hadn't been announced yet. No qualification framework generates that question. Silence generates it.

The champion coaching the rep, live, without meaning to

Three separate calls had a second buyer-side attendee visibly typing, or audible, prompting the primary buyer with better questions than the rep was asking. In one, the internal champion actually said, half under her breath but on a live mic, "ask him about the API thing": feeding her own boss the exact question that would eventually surface the deal-killer, three calls earlier than it actually did.

This is a category the standard vendor-versus-buyer framing has no room for: the champion who is, functionally, running discovery on the rep's behalf, better than the rep is running it themselves. Reps who aren't listening for who in the room is doing the real qualifying work miss the one person on the call who's actually on their side. Recognising and working with that person is its own skill, the Champion Identification Checklist is the closest thing I've seen to a starting point for teaching it deliberately rather than leaving it to instinct.

"Just gathering information" is never true

Eight buyers opened with some version of "we're just gathering information, nothing's decided yet." In every one of those eight calls, something had already been decided: a shortlist existed, a budget line existed, a date existed, and it surfaced within the first fifteen minutes regardless of the opening disclaimer. The phrase isn't information. It's a social lubricant buyers use to lower the temperature of a call they're worried will turn pushy. Reps who took it literally and eased off qualifying accordingly missed material that was sitting in plain sight two questions later.

The stapled-together call

Roughly a third of the sample weren't one discovery call: they were two, badly stapled together. The first eight to ten minutes covered the stated problem the buyer had booked the call to talk about. Then, somewhere in the second half, a different, usually more urgent problem surfaced almost by accident, in response to a question that wasn't aimed at finding it. "Oh, that reminds me, we're also dealing with..." Reps who'd already mentally filed the call under the first problem frequently didn't register the pivot; a few asked one follow-up question and then steered straight back to their script, leaving the live wound unexamined for the rest of the call.

What no framework captures: the first ninety seconds

The single strongest predictor across all forty calls of whether the rest of the call would go well had nothing to do with any question asked. It was tone, set in the first ninety seconds, before qualification technique of any kind came into play: specifically, whether the rep sounded like they were interviewing the buyer or talking with them. You can run a textbook MEDDIC sequence in a tone that makes a buyer clam up, and you can run almost no structure at all in a tone that gets a buyer to hand you the whole deal for free. I've yet to see a qualification framework that scores for this, because it isn't a question you ask. It's closer to a register you're in before you've asked anything.

What this is actually for

None of this replaces BANT or MEDDIC or CHAMP as a scaffold. If you want to see how they actually differ rather than argue about which acronym wins, the Qualification Framework Comparison Guide (BANT vs. MEDDIC vs. CHAMP) lays it out plainly, and reps who freelance discovery with no structure at all do worse, not better. But a scaffold tells you what to ask. It doesn't tell you what to do with eleven seconds of silence, a buyer who's already told you everything, or a champion coaching your rep from the buyer's side of the table. That part isn't in the framework. It's in the forty calls, and the four hundred after them, and whether anyone with a notebook: or a Discovery Call Scorecard for Managers, if you'd rather have somewhere to write it down: was actually paying attention to what happened, rather than to what the checklist expected to find.

The part worth sitting with

Every one of these patterns showed up more than once, across reps who'd never worked together, selling different products, into different industries. That's not a coincidence you can shrug off as "every deal is different." It means discovery has a shape that exists independently of any single company's playbook: and most of that shape is currently unwritten down anywhere, taught by nobody, and left to whichever manager happens to be listening that quarter.
