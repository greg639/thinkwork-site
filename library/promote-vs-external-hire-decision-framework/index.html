Promote-from-Within vs. External Hire Decision Framework

A weighted scoring framework that turns 'gut feel' into a defensible, repeatable decision the next time you're choosing between developing your best rep into a manager and hiring an experienced leader from outside.

How to use it

Score both candidates independently across all 8 criteria before comparing notes, check the hard-stop list first, then use the gap size to decide how much weight to give the numeric total versus the tie-break question.

What's inside

  • 8 weighted decision criteria (100 points total) with 1–5 behavioral scoring anchors
  • Side-by-side scoring worksheet for internal vs. external candidates
  • Gap-size interpretation rules (15+, 5–14, under 5 points)
  • A tie-break question for true toss-ups
  • 5 hard-stop disqualifiers that override the score
  • A fully worked numeric example
  • Scripts for communicating the decision to the path not chosen

Use this when: you have an open (or about-to-open) sales management seat and at least one credible internal candidate.

Step 1: Score both paths on the same 8 criteria

For each criterion, score 1–5 (anchors below), multiply by weight ÷ 5, and sum.

Formula per row: (score ÷ 5) × weight = weighted points. Sum the weighted points column per candidate.

Scoring anchors

1. Demonstrated coaching behavior today (weight 20: the single biggest predictor of first-year manager success)

  • 1 = Never coaches peers; keeps knowledge/technique to themselves
  • 3 = Helps peers when asked; hasn't proactively developed anyone
  • 5 = Has proactively, repeatedly coached at least one peer with a visible before/after improvement, unprompted

(External candidates: score based on specific, verifiable examples from their last role, not "I love developing people" as a claim.)

2. Credibility & trust with this specific team (weight 15)

  • 1 = Team would see this person/hire as illegitimate or resented
  • 3 = Neutral: team has no strong opinion either way
  • 5 = Team already looks to this person for guidance, or an external hire brings category-defining credibility

3. Culture-reset need vs. continuity need (weight 15) Score this for the situation, then match: if the real problem is burnout from a prior manager or a stalled culture, weight toward external (fresh authority, no baggage). If the team is winning and just needs structure, weight toward internal (continuity).

  • 1 = Situation clearly favors the other path
  • 3 = Either path plausible
  • 5 = Situation clearly favors this path

4. Time-to-productivity / ramp risk (weight 10)

  • 1 = 6+ months to full effectiveness in the seat
  • 3 = 3–4 months
  • 5 = Under 8 weeks

5. Operational & forecasting rigor already shown (weight 10)

  • 1 = No evidence of forecast discipline, CRM hygiene, or process ownership
  • 3 = Personally disciplined but has never run a process for others
  • 5 = Has run a forecast, pipeline review, or process for a team with evidence it worked

6. Track record / ceiling already proven (weight 10)

  • 1 = Below-average individual performer
  • 3 = Solid, consistent performer
  • 5 = Top performer with a track record that gives the team an immediate "they've earned it" reaction

7. Flight risk & bench-morale cost if passed over (weight 10)

  • 1 = No risk
  • 3 = Some risk, candidate might disengage
  • 5 = High risk, passing over this person visibly damages retention of your other top performers who are watching

8. Total cost (weight 10): comp premium, backfill of the vacated IC seat, relocation, sign-on bonus, recruiter fee

  • 1 = Materially more expensive path
  • 3 = Roughly comparable
  • 5 = Materially cheaper path

Step 2, Read the gap

  • Gap of 15+ points: Take the higher-scoring path.
  • Gap of 5–14 points: Re-examine criterion #1 and #3 specifically: if those two individually favor a different path than your total, trust #1 and #3; coaching behavior and situational fit are harder to fake or coach around than the rest.
  • Gap under 5 points (true toss-up): Use the tie-break rule below.

Tie-break rule

Ask one question: "If this hire fails in 6 months, which failure can we recover from faster?" An internal promotion that fails can usually be walked back with the person staying on the team in their old seat. An external hire that fails usually means restarting the entire search, plus a demoralized team who watched their manager fail and now the internal candidate they were passed over for still isn't in the seat. When truly tied, this recoverability argument should break the tie toward internal.

Hard-stop disqualifiers (override the score)

  1. Internal: Active, unresolved performance or conduct issue in the last 6 months.
  2. External: Cannot describe their coaching philosophy with one specific, verifiable example when asked directly.
  3. Internal: The only argument for this person is "nobody else wants the job": a default promotion, not an earned one.
  4. External: Hiring them requires restructuring existing reps' territory or comp in a way that hasn't been communicated to those reps yet.
  5. Either: Your last two engagement or exit-interview data points already show the team doesn't trust management, and this decision doesn't address why.

Worked example

A 9-person SaaS AE team. Internal candidate: strong coaching signal (mentored 2 new hires, both hit ramp early), high team trust, situation is continuity (team is winning), but zero forecasting/process experience and would need to backfill her own $1.4M quota seat.

External candidate: built a near-identical motion at a competitor, strong forecasting background, but team has never met them and two reps have said in 1:1s they'd be uneasy with an outsider given how tight the team is.

What to say to the path not chosen

To an internal candidate you didn't promote: "You were a real candidate, not a placeholder: here's specifically what put [name] ahead this time [name the 1–2 top-weighted criteria], and here's the plan to close those gaps for you in the next 6 months so this isn't a one-time answer."

To an external finalist you didn't hire: "We went internal this round because of team continuity, not because of anything short in your background: we'd like to keep the door open for [specific future scenario, e.g., a second manager seat opening in Q_]."
