The Manager Skill Nobody Puts on a Job Description: Diagnosing Before Prescribing

Every job description for 'sales manager' lists coaching as a bullet point. None of them explain that the actual skill is diagnosis: and diagnosis is learnable in a specific, trainable way.

Every sales manager job description says the same three things: quota ownership, forecast accuracy, and coaching and development of the team. The first two come with numbers attached, you hit them or you don't. The third is a bullet point with nothing behind it, an assumption that if you closed well yourself and people generally like you, you'll be fine at developing other people to close well too. That assumption is why so many strong reps turn into average managers within eighteen months and can't work out what went wrong. Coaching isn't a personality trait. It's a specific, learnable skill, and the actual skill, the one nobody writes into the job description because nobody's broken it down far enough to name it, is diagnosis. Figuring out which one of fifty-plus underlying competencies is actually missing, before you say a word of advice.

"Coaching ability" is not a skill, it's a proxy

When companies hire or promote for coaching ability, they're almost never measuring coaching. They're measuring proxies: does this person seem approachable, do their current peers speak well of them, have they been around long enough to have "earned" the seat. None of those proxies predict whether someone can sit across from a rep who's stuck, listen to one call, and correctly identify that the problem isn't confidence and isn't product knowledge: it's that the rep skips confirming the economic buyer before he pitches price, every single time, and has been doing it for a year without anyone naming it.

That's diagnosis. It's a distinct cognitive skill from the skill of selling, which is why plenty of excellent sellers are mediocre diagnosticians of other people's selling, and why, less intuitively, some middling sellers turn out to be sharp diagnosticians the moment you hand them a real taxonomy to check against.

What diagnosis actually requires

Three things, none of which show up on a typical manager scorecard:

  1. A reference taxonomy. You cannot name a gap in a skill you haven't defined. If your organisation's idea of sales competency stops at "discovery," "objection handling" and "closing," your diagnostic resolution stops there too, everything gets filed under one of three buckets regardless of what actually happened.
  2. Pattern recognition across observations, not verdicts from one call. One bad call has a dozen plausible explanations. The same failure showing up in the third, sixth and ninth call is a competency gap, not a bad day. Diagnosis is a longitudinal skill; most 1:1s are run as if it's a snapshot skill.
  3. The discipline to withhold prescription until diagnosis is done. The natural move for anyone who's sold well themselves is to hear a symptom and immediately reach for what worked for them. That's prescription without diagnosis, and it's the single most common failure mode in sales coaching, advice that's correct in general and wrong for this specific rep's specific gap.

The skill at each career stage

Diagnosis doesn't switch on the day you get a manager title. It's already the skill separating a good individual contributor from a great one, and it keeps compounding in scope all the way to VP.

StageWhat diagnosis looks likeCommon failure mode at this stage
Rep (IC1–IC3)Diagnosing your own losses: naming the specific competency behind a lost deal instead of blaming the market, the price, or the lead qualityAttributing every loss to external factors, so no internal skill ever gets flagged for practice
New frontline managerDiagnosing one rep at a time, across multiple calls, against a shared taxonomy rather than your own playbookPrescribing what worked for you as a rep, regardless of whether it matches this rep's actual gap
Senior managerDiagnosing patterns across a team: three reps failing the same competency is a team-level training gap, not three individual coaching conversationsTreating a systemic gap as three unrelated performance issues, coaching each in isolation
DirectorDiagnosing whether the managers underneath you can diagnose: auditing their coaching, not just their numbersJudging managers purely on team quota attainment, which rewards inherited pipeline quality as much as coaching skill
VP / CRODiagnosing whether the organisation's competency taxonomy itself is still accurate: coaching people on skills that still matter, not last cycle's playbookAssuming the coaching system works because revenue is up, when revenue is up for reasons that have nothing to do with coaching quality

Notice what doesn't change as you go down that list: the core act is always "find the specific, correct cause before you act." What changes is the unit you're diagnosing: yourself, one person, a team, a layer of managers, a whole taxonomy. If you can't do it at the rep level, promoting you doesn't make you able to do it at the team level. It just makes the blast radius of a wrong diagnosis bigger.

How to build the skill before anyone gives you the title

You don't need a direct report to start practising diagnosis. You need reps' worth of calls, your own or peers', and a habit of writing down the specific competency before you write down the fix. Use a real taxonomy rather than inventing categories on the fly; the Sales Career Level Matrix (IC1 → Principal/Enterprise) is a reasonable place to see what's actually expected to differ stage to stage, so you're not diagnosing against a blank page.

When you do get your first direct report, resist the urge to hand out advice in the moment. Structure the conversation so diagnosis has to happen before prescription does: the GROW Model Coaching Conversation Script forces the "what's actually going on" question before the "here's what to do" question, which is the entire point. And when you give the feedback, run it through something like the Constructive Feedback Cheat Sheet for Sales Managers so the specificity of your diagnosis doesn't get lost in vague encouragement on the way out of your mouth.

Coaching ability isn't a trait some managers have and others don't. It's a skill with a name, and now that you've got the name, you can go and get good at it before somebody hands you the job title that assumes you already are.
