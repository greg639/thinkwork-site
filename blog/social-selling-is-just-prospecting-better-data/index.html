Stop Calling It 'Social Selling.' It's Prospecting With Better Data, and Treating It Separately Makes Reps Worse at Both.

LinkedIn outreach gets its own training track, its own metrics, its own guru circuit, as if the underlying skill isn't identical to picking up the phone.

A client asked me last month why their "social selling" numbers were up 40% while pipeline sourced from outbound was flat. I asked what social selling meant at their org. The answer was: reps posting on LinkedIn, commenting on prospects' posts, and sending connection requests with a note. I asked what was in the note. It was the same opening line their cold-call script used, badly translated into text. They'd built an entire second training track, a separate metric, a separate coach, and a separate Tuesday all-hands slot for a discipline that was, on inspection, the same skill their cold callers already had, executed worse, because nobody had told the reps that.

This is the part nobody in enablement wants to say out loud: "social selling" is not a discipline. It's a channel. The skill underneath it: qualifying a stranger's relevance to you, and yours to them, fast enough that they don't disengage, is identical to the skill underneath a cold call. Split the training and you don't get two disciplines done well. You get one skill trained twice, badly, at double the cost.

The tell is in what the training decks actually teach

Pull up a "social selling" curriculum and a cold-calling curriculum from the same enablement team and lay them side by side. You'll find the same content wearing different clothes.

Every row on the right is the same competency, wearing a different hat for a different medium. Nobody is teaching a new skill in the "social selling" column. They're teaching the same skill again, with worse feedback loops, because a LinkedIn message doesn't let you hear tone and a cold call doesn't let you screenshot a mutual connection.

Where the theatre creeps in

LinkedIn's Social Selling Index is the clearest piece of theatre in the whole discipline. It rewards profile completeness, network size, and posting frequency, none of which correlate with a rep's ability to get a stranger to respond to an outbound message. I've sat across from reps with SSI scores in the 80s who couldn't get a 10% reply rate on cold outreach, because the score measures performing activity on the platform, not the underlying skill of writing a message a busy stranger wants to answer. Enablement teams that report SSI improvement to leadership as a proxy for pipeline health are reporting a number that measures LinkedIn usage, dressed up as a sales metric.

Engagement pods are the second piece of theatre, groups of reps who like and comment on each other's posts to game the algorithm's reach. It produces impressive-looking activity dashboards and zero qualified pipeline, because the "engagement" isn't coming from prospects. It's coming from other reps pretending to be an audience.

None of this is an argument against LinkedIn as a channel. It's an argument against training reps to perform on a channel instead of training the skill that makes any channel work.

What actually differs by channel, and how much it should change your tactics

There are real differences between phone and social. They're mechanical, not skill-level, and they should change your tactics in proportion to how much they actually affect outcomes: which, ranked, is less than most training decks imply.

  1. Response latency tolerance, the biggest real difference. A cold call resolves in the moment; a LinkedIn message can sit unread for days. This genuinely changes cadence design: how long you wait before a follow-up: and deserves its own line in your outbound cadence, not its own competency framework.
  1. Proof format: on the phone, tone of voice carries credibility; in a DM, a specific detail from their profile or a mutual connection carries it instead. This changes what "doing your homework" looks like in practice, but it's the same qualifying-relevance skill expressed through a different proof mechanism, not a new skill.
  1. Permission norms for follow-up frequency, a third call in one day reads as harassment; a third LinkedIn touch across a week reads as normal cadence. Ranked lowest because it's the easiest to codify into a cadence rule and requires no separate training, just a different number in your outreach playbook.

None of these three earns a separate training track, a separate coach, or a separate KPI dashboard. They earn three lines in your Multichannel Outbound Cadence Framework.

What to actually build instead

Stop training "social selling" as a discipline. Train the underlying competency: qualifying a stranger's relevance fast, under time pressure, with limited information: and teach the channel-specific mechanics as a thirty-minute appendix, not a parallel curriculum. Grade a rep's DM opener and their phone opener against the same rubric, because they should be assessed on the same skill. If your best cold caller writes a mediocre LinkedIn message, that's not a "different skill" gap: that's the same skill, unpractised in a new format, and it'll close faster than a six-week "social selling bootcamp" ever will.

Reps who need a starting point on the mechanics of each channel without pretending they're separate disciplines can pull from the LinkedIn Cold Outreach Message Swipe File and the Cold Call Opener Swipe File side by side: read them as the same structure twice, not two different playbooks.

The real cost of the split

Every hour your enablement calendar spends on a "social selling" module that could have been a footnote in cold-call training is an hour not spent getting a rep good at the one thing that actually moves both numbers: reading a stranger fast enough to earn the next thirty seconds of their attention. Merge the training. You'll get better at both channels by teaching one skill properly instead of a fake skill twice.
