Competitor Battlecard Template Directory

A curated directory of 8 battlecard structures worth stealing for your next competitive deal review, plus one fully fleshed-out fill-in-the-blank template you can use today.

Eight battlecard structures, what each is actually good for, and where each one falls apart if it's the only format you use — followed by one complete template to fill in.

1. Head-to-Head Feature Grid

Structure: rows of features, columns of vendors, checkmarks or ratings per cell. Good for: RFP responses and procurement-driven evaluations where a checklist genuinely gets read. Falls apart when: used in live sales conversations — it invites a feature-by-feature argument you can lose on a single row, and it says nothing about outcomes.

2. Landmine / Trap-Setting Battlecard

Structure: a list of discovery questions planted earlier in the sales cycle that make the competitor's weakness surface naturally later, without you ever naming them. Good for: competitive deals where you get to run discovery before the prospect brings the competitor up. Falls apart when: you're brought in after the prospect already has a competitor's proposal in hand — there's no "earlier" left to plant anything in.

3. Objection-Response Battlecard

Structure: a table of the specific things reps hear prospects say about a competitor, paired with a word-for-word response. Good for: onboarding new reps fast and keeping messaging consistent across a team. Falls apart when: the responses read as scripted and defensive rather than confident — reps need to internalize the substance, not just recite the line.

4. Win/Loss Narrative Battlecard

Structure: short real anecdotes — "here's a deal we won against them and why," "here's one we lost and why" — instead of static claims. Good for: building rep conviction; stories are more persuasive internally and externally than bullet claims. Falls apart when: it isn't refreshed — a stale win/loss story from 18 months ago on an outdated competitor version does more harm than good.

5. Why-We-Win / Why-We-Lose One-Pager

Structure: two columns — the conditions under which you reliably beat this competitor, and the conditions under which you reliably lose to them. Good for: deal qualification early — deciding whether to compete hard or walk away. Falls apart when: the "why we lose" column is left blank out of ego — an honest battlecard needs both columns to be useful.

6. Persona-Specific Battlecard

Structure: the same competitor, broken into separate one-pagers per buyer persona (economic buyer, technical evaluator, end user) since each cares about different differentiators. Good for: enterprise deals with multiple stakeholders who will each research the competitor separately. Falls apart when: a small team doesn't have the bandwidth to maintain 3-4 versions per competitor — better to have one great generic version than four half-maintained ones.

7. Pricing & Packaging Comparison

Structure: side-by-side breakdown of how pricing models differ (per-seat vs. usage-based, what's bundled vs. add-on, contract terms), not just headline price. Good for: deals where the prospect's real objection is total cost of ownership, not sticker price. Falls apart when: competitor pricing isn't public and gets stale fast — needs a clear "last verified" date or it misleads reps.

8. Migration/Displacement Battlecard

Structure: specifically for deals where the prospect already uses the competitor — covers switching costs, data migration effort, contract-timing tactics, and "what breaks during the switch." Good for: competitive displacement / renewal-timed deals. Falls apart when: used for greenfield deals where the prospect has never touched the competitor — the switching-cost content is irrelevant noise there.

The combined template — fill this in per competitor

```markdown

Battlecard: [Your Company] vs. [Competitor Name]

Last verified: [date] — pricing and positioning change; recheck quarterly.

30-Second Positioning

[One sentence: when a prospect mentions this competitor, say this.]

Why We Win

  • [Condition 1 where we reliably beat them]
  • [Condition 2]
  • [Condition 3]

Why We Lose (be honest)

  • [Condition 1 where they reliably beat us]
  • [Condition 2]

Landmine Questions (ask in discovery, before the competitor is named)

  • "[Question that surfaces their known weakness naturally]"
  • "[Question 2]"

Objection → Response

What prospects sayWhat to say back
"[Objection heard in the field]""[Word-for-word response]"
"[Objection 2]""[Response 2]"

Pricing & Packaging Notes

[Model type, what's bundled vs. add-on, contract length norms — flagged with last-verified date]

Migration/Displacement Notes (if prospect already uses them)

[Switching cost, data migration effort, contract timing tactic]

Real Win Story

[Short anecdote: deal we won against them, what tipped it]

Real Loss Story

[Short anecdote: deal we lost to them, what tipped it — and what we'd do differently] ```

How to use it

Pick the 2-3 structures that match how your team actually competes (most teams need the Why-We-Win/Lose one-pager plus one tactical format), then fill in the combined template per competitor and put a review date on it — a battlecard with no last-verified date will mislead reps within two quarters.
