I Read 12 Sales Enablement Job Postings From Public Companies. This Is What They Actually Pay For.

Twelve real job postings for enablement roles at public companies, read line by line for comp band, reporting line, and listed KPIs, tell you what a company actually believes enablement is for.

Every enablement job posting opens with the same paragraph. "Strategic partner to the revenue organisation." "Drive transformational impact across the go-to-market motion." "Own the enablement vision." I've hired for this role three times and written that paragraph myself, so I don't say this to sneer at it — I say it because that paragraph tells you nothing. The mission statement at the top of a job posting is marketing copy written to attract candidates. The reporting line, the comp band, and the actual KPI listed three screens down are written by finance and the hiring manager, and they don't lie. I pulled twelve live postings for enablement roles at public companies — a spread from a mid-cap SaaS vendor to a multi-billion-pound industrial conglomerate, identified here by profile rather than name — and read past the mission paragraph on all twelve. Here's what they actually pay for.

The reporting line is the tell before you even reach the KPIs

Four reporting lines show up across twelve postings, and each one predicts almost everything else in the listing before you get to the metrics — ranked here by how much real strategic weight the role actually carries, not by how senior the title sounds:

  1. Reports to CRO/CCO. Three postings, and the highest comp bands in the sample. These are the only roles where the KPI section talks about win rate and quota attainment in the same breath as ramp time — meaning the company has decided enablement owns a number, not just a curriculum. This sits top because it's the only reporting line where enablement is accountable for a revenue outcome rather than an activity.
  2. Reports to VP Sales. Four postings, second-highest bands. Genuine performance function, but scoped to onboarding and ramp rather than full skill development — a real seat, just a narrower mandate than the CRO-owned version.
  3. Reports to Marketing. Two postings, and the KPIs give it away instantly: downloads, engagement, webinar attendance. This ranks below the sales-owned roles because the KPIs measure content consumption, not behaviour change — a content-operations role that inherited the enablement title somewhere along the way, most likely during a reorg.
  4. Reports to HR/L&D or Sales Ops. Three postings, the lowest bands. KPIs are activity metrics — hours logged, completion rates, tool adoption — not outcome metrics. This ranks last because the function is compliance or operations, useful in its own right, but nothing in the KPI line ties it to skill or performance.

The mismatch worth naming

Eight of the twelve postings open with strategic-partner language regardless of which reporting line and KPI set followed it. The mission paragraph is nearly identical whether the role reports to a CRO with a win-rate target or to L&D with a completion-rate target. That's the real finding here: the front of a posting is written to attract the calibre of candidate the company wants to believe it's hiring, and the back of the posting — reporting line, comp, KPI — is written by whoever controls the budget and reflects what the company is actually willing to fund. If you're evaluating a role, read the mission paragraph last. Read the reporting line and the KPI first, because those are the two fields nobody polishes for the job board.

What this means if you're hiring, or being hired

If you're writing the posting, the KPI you list is a public commitment about what the function is for — list ramp time and win rate only if you're prepared to fund the coaching cadence and manager involvement that actually move them, not just a content library. Benchmark the seniority you're offering against what the role is genuinely accountable for using something sharper than a market-rate guess — the Sales Leadership Competency Scorecard is a faster gut-check than another round of comp surveys. If you're not sure whether the function as designed should report to sales, marketing, or ops, that's an org design question, and the Sales Org Design Blueprint will force the conversation before the posting goes live rather than after the wrong hire starts.

If you're the candidate, ignore the first paragraph entirely and go straight to the KPI line. It's the only sentence in the posting written by someone with a budget to defend, and the only one that tells you what you'll actually be measured against in month four. A GTM & Sales Leadership Metrics Cheat Sheet is worth having open next to the posting — half the KPI language in these listings is borrowed from a metrics glossary nobody on the hiring panel fully understands themselves.

Companies don't lie about what enablement is for. They just say it twice, in two different registers, three screens apart — and only one of those two tells the truth.
