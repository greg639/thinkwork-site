There's No Career Ladder Inside Enablement Yet, So I Built One

There's no agreed job-leveling structure inside enablement: no comp bands, no defined skill bar between an associate and a director: so here's a proposed ladder, built the way sales orgs already build theirs.

A sales org can tell you, to the pound, what separates an SDR from an Account Executive, and what separates a first-year AE from someone eligible for a Senior title. Quota size, deal complexity, ramp expectations, comp bands: all documented, all defensible in a comp review. Ask the same org what separates an Enablement Associate from an Enablement Director, and you'll get a shrug, a title that means something different at every company that uses it, and a comp band that was set by whatever the hiring manager could get past finance that quarter. I've hired into enablement roles with three different titles at three companies doing near-identical work, and been paid three different amounts for it. That's not a market being efficient. That's a market with no ladder.

Why this gap actually costs you

It's not just an annoyance for the people in the seats. It shows up as a business problem in three specific ways.

First, headcount planning becomes guesswork. If nobody can say what a "Senior Enablement Manager" does differently than an "Enablement Manager," you can't build a rational team structure: you end up hiring generically labelled bodies against vague need, and the org chart bloats sideways instead of growing a genuine skill bench.

Second, retention leaks at exactly the point you can least afford it. The people who get good at this job: genuinely good, not just busy, hit a ceiling around 18 months where the only visible next step is "manager of enablement people" even if they have zero interest in managing people and every interest in getting better at the craft. They leave for a title bump at a company with an equally undefined ladder, and the cycle repeats.

Third, and this is the one leadership actually feels: without levels, there's no way to price the function. Every enablement hire becomes a fresh negotiation with no comparable inside the building, which means you either overpay to avoid a fight or underpay and lose the candidate to a company that will.

The ladder, level by level

This is the structure I use: built the same way a sales org builds its own ladder, by defining what changes at each level: scope of ownership, not years served.

LevelTitleWhat they ownSkill bar to get thereIndicative UK comp band (base)
1Enablement AssociateContent delivery against an existing curriculum; logistics for training and onboarding cohortsCan run a session someone else designed, competently, without supervision£32k–£42k
2Enablement ManagerOwns one program end to end (e.g. onboarding, or a single skill curriculum) including its measurementCan design a curriculum from a stated skill gap, and can show a before/after number for it£45k–£58k
3Senior Enablement ManagerOwns a portfolio of programs across a full rep lifecycle segment (e.g. everything pre-ramp, or everything mid-tenure)Can prioritise across competing program requests using business impact, not squeaky-wheel volume£58k–£72k
4Director of EnablementOwns the enablement roadmap and its budget; sits in the QBR as a stakeholder, not a presenterCan forecast the cost of not funding a program in terms the CRO already tracks (see: every budget conversation you've ever lost)£75k–£95k
5VP EnablementOwns a P&L line or a shared accountability for a revenue number (ramp-to-quota time, win rate uplift, retention of a cohort)Has already done this once, or has the political capital to be handed the number before proving it£100k–£140k+

The jump that matters most is between level 3 and level 4, and it's not a management jump, plenty of Directors in this ladder have no direct reports. It's a jump from owning programs to owning a forecast. That's the same jump enablement leaders have to make to get budget approved in the first place, which is not a coincidence; it's the same skill measured two different ways.

Where this differs from just copying the sales ladder

The temptation is to lift the AE ladder wholesale: SDR, AE, Senior AE, Team Lead, Manager, Director, VP, and relabel it. Don't. The AE ladder scales on quota size and deal complexity, both externally verifiable numbers. Enablement doesn't have an equivalent external number at the associate and manager levels, so the ladder has to scale on scope of ownership and evidence of impact instead: which is a harder thing to assess consistently, and exactly why most companies give up and just use "years of experience" as a proxy. Years of experience is a lazy proxy. Someone who has run the same onboarding module for four years without changing it is not more senior than someone who redesigned a curriculum and moved a ramp-time number in eighteen months.

If you're building this ladder for your own team, the mechanism that saves you from years-of-experience laziness is the same one that saves you in a budget conversation: attach a measurable output to each level, not a tenure requirement. A useful reference point is how sales orgs already map this kind of progression externally, the SDR-to-CRO Career Path Map is a decent model for the shape of a ladder even though the content doesn't transfer directly.

What to do with this on Monday

  1. Write down what "Manager" currently means at your company, concretely, in terms of what a person in that seat owns: not what they're good at, what they're accountable for.
  2. Find the person who's been stuck at one level for two-plus years and ask them, honestly, what they'd need to own to justify the next one. Their answer will be more accurate than any framework, including this one.
  3. Attach a number to each level, not a responsibility. "Owns onboarding" is not a level. "Owns onboarding and can show a ramp-time delta" is.
  4. Use a real job description, not a title, when you're hiring into any of these seats, a Sales Job Description Library (10 Roles) is built for quota-carrying roles but the discipline of writing scope-before-title transfers directly.
  5. Build the growth plan before the promotion conversation, not after, an Individual Development Plan (IDP) Template for Sales Reps adapts cleanly to enablement seats if you swap the quota metrics for the program-impact metrics in the table above.

The part nobody wants to hear

The reason this ladder doesn't exist industry-wide isn't that it's hard to design. It's the one above, it took me an afternoon. It's that most enablement leaders haven't been given the standing to define what "senior" means in their own function, because the function itself hasn't been given a P&L to justify the definition. Fix that, and the ladder mostly writes itself.
