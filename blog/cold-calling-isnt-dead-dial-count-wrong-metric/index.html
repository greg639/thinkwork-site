Cold Calling Isn't Dead. Your Dial Count Is Just Measuring the Wrong Thing.

The debate about whether cold calling still works is a distraction from the one that matters: most outbound programs are optimizing for a metric that has nothing to do with skill.

Every eighteen months or so, someone reheats the "cold calling is dead" argument, usually while selling you the tool that's supposed to replace it. It isn't dead. I watched a rep close a £40,000 annual deal off a cold dial three weeks ago — no inbound signal, no warm intro, just a phone number and about forty seconds of a decent opener. What's actually broken isn't the channel. It's the number most outbound teams use to run it: dial count. Chase dials hard enough and you'll hit target some weeks and miss it badly on others, and nobody in the building will be able to tell you why — because dial count doesn't measure the one variable that decides whether a call goes anywhere. It measures how fast someone can get through a list.

The volume religion

Dial count became the default metric for a boring reason: it's the easiest thing to see on a dashboard. Every dialer logs it automatically, every manager can screenshot it in a 1:1, every VP can put it on a slide next to a target. None of that makes it causal. It's an activity proxy that got promoted to a KPI because measuring the thing that actually matters — what happens in the first four seconds after "hello" — requires someone to sit down and listen to calls, which is slower and less flattering than counting keystrokes on a dialer.

The result is an org that watches something with a weak relationship to revenue very closely, and pays almost no attention to something with a strong one. I've graded outbound calls across a handful of B2B teams, and the pattern repeats itself: reps hitting 70-plus dials a day with a flat, apologetic, or over-scripted opener convert those dials into real conversations at roughly a third the rate of reps doing 40 dials a day with a sharp one. The high-volume rep looks better on the activity report. The low-volume rep books more meetings. Nobody in leadership notices, because leadership is reading the dial column, not listening to the calls.

What dial count can't see

Here's what actually decides a cold call's fate: whether the prospect gives you a second sentence. Not whether they buy, not whether they book a meeting on that call — whether, after your opening line, they respond with something other than a reflexive brush-off. That decision gets made in under five seconds, and it's driven entirely by craft: pacing, a direct acknowledgement that this is an unscheduled interruption, and a reason for the call specific enough that hanging up costs the prospect a flicker of curiosity.

None of that shows up in a dial count. Two reps can dial the same 65 numbers on a Tuesday and produce wildly different outcomes:

Rep A (volume-first)Rep B (opener-first)
Dials6841
Connects1412
Second-sentence responses49
Meetings booked13

Rep A's activity report looks stronger on the metric that gets rewarded. Rep B books three times the meetings from fewer than two-thirds the dials. If your comp plan, your SPIFFs, and your 1:1s are all built around the left-hand column, you are actively coaching your team toward Rep A's behaviour.

The maths sales leaders refuse to run

I understand why leaders default to dial count. Coaching volume is easy — "make more calls" requires no listening, no rubric, no judgment call about what's actually wrong. Coaching the opener requires sitting through calls, some of them uncomfortable, and telling a rep that their tone in the first ten words is costing them the conversation. That's a harder conversation to have in a 1:1, so most managers don't have it. They default to the number that's already sitting in the dashboard.

The uncomfortable maths: if opener quality swings second-sentence rate by two to three times between reps making the same volume of calls, then every hour spent pushing a below-target dial count toward the team average is worth less than fifteen minutes spent fixing one rep's first ten seconds. Most outbound programmes invest the ratio backwards — hours of activity management for minutes of skill coaching.

What to measure instead

You don't need to throw out dial count. You need to stop treating it as the metric that matters and start tracking the one that's actually diagnostic: second-sentence rate, the share of connected calls where the prospect responds with a real sentence rather than an immediate objection or hang-up. It's gradable, it's coachable, and unlike dial count, it tells you exactly where to intervene.

Three changes worth making this quarter:

  1. Grade a sample of connected calls weekly against an opener rubric, not just a dial log. You're looking for a specific, repeatable behaviour, not "energy" or "confidence," which are too vague to coach against. A structured Call Monitoring & Coaching Scorecard turns "that call felt off" into a rubric line you can actually train to.
  2. Set targets on conversations, not dials, and let dial volume float as a background metric management watches quietly rather than a number reps get pushed to hit at the expense of quality.
  3. Tie contests and SPIFFs to conversion, not speed. A leaderboard built on dial count rewards exactly the behaviour — rushing the opener to get to the next number — that's costing you meetings. If you want a shared language for where a rep sits on opener craft, the Cold Calling Skill Level Rubric (Novice to Master) gives you bands to coach toward instead of a single pass/fail bar.

If you want the blunter version: stop debating whether cold calling works, and run the Cold Call Conversion Rate Calculator on your own team's numbers, split by opener grade rather than by rep tenure or territory. You'll find the channel is fine. Your coaching just hasn't been pointed at it.

Dial count was never the metric. It was the proxy we reached for because coaching craft is harder than counting keystrokes. Grade the opener instead of the dialer, and the "is cold calling dead" debate stops being a debate at all — it was never about the channel.
